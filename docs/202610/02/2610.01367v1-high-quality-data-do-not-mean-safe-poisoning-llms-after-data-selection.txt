Abstract 

Safety-aligned Large Language Models remain vulnerable to fine-tuning on small sets of harmful or benign-looking sam-ples. However, prior studies typically assume that poisoned samples directly enter downstream fine-tuning, overlooking quality-based selection in practical training pipelines. To fill this gap, we systematically evaluate both the filtering effects against poisoning and the downstream safety impact of re-tained data. The results reveal that selection removes many overtly harmful samples, yet some retained high-quality sam-ples can still degrade model safety alignment possibly due to their harmful-like training-update patterns at the layer-wise gradient level. Together, these findings expose a practi-cal vulnerability: safety-degrading influence can pass through quality-based selection via retained high-quality samples. To examine its systematic exploitability, we propose Bi -Stage 

Quality-Constrained Safety-Degradation Text Optimization (Bi-QSTO ), which optimizes poisoned samples under an ex-plicit quality constraint to survive selection while preserving their safety-degrading influence. Across poisoning settings, target models, and filtering rates, Bi-QSTO maintains attack effectiveness before and after selection. Even at 90% filtering, harmful-seeded samples achieve a Poisoning Retention Rate above 90% and Harmful Score of 3.30–4.01. Their attack ef-fectiveness strongly transfers across models and their retention advantage generalizes to additional selection methods. 

## Introduction 

LLMs are widely deployed in real-world applications and typically undergo various forms of safety alignment before deployment (Zhao et al. 2023; Ouyang et al. 2022). How-ever, downstream fine-tuning can weaken or override these safeguards because model behavior remains highly depen-dent on fine-tuning data (Yi et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2025; Grattafiori et al. 2024). This risk is amplified by data poison-ing, where malicious samples steer parameter updates away from aligned behavior and increase compliance with harmful instructions (Ji et al. 2025b, 2023; Qi et al. 2024; Huang et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2026). Such fine-tuning-induced safety degradation is referred to as a fine-tuning jailbreak . Impor-tantly, poisoning samples need not be explicitly harmful; even benign-looking samples can undermine model safety while evading content-based inspection (Qi et al. 2024; Guan et al.

Many publicly documented LLM training pipelines com-bine quality-based data selection with content-safety moder-ation to retain data with higher textual quality while filtering harmful, sensitive, or policy-violating content (Albalak et al. 2024; Penedo et al. 2024; Qiu et al. 2024; Maini et al. 2025; Shen et al. 2025; Grattafiori et al. 2024; OpenAI 2025). Among these procedures, quality-based data selection seeks to improve model performance by preferentially retaining samples assigned relatively higher quality scores by the se-lector (Zhang et al. 2025; Li et al. 2024; Wettig et al. 2024; Peng et al. 2025). Such selection determines which samples enter downstream fine-tuning based on their quality scores, making retention a prerequisite for poisoning to affect the target model. Prior poisoning studies primarily improve at-tack effectiveness or stealth while assuming direct inclusion of poisoned samples in the training corpus (Qi et al. 2024; Guan et al. 2025). Meanwhile, some studies treat data se-lection only as a preliminary mitigation, without examining poisoning survival and post-selection effectiveness in practi-cal training pipelines (Fu et al. 2025; Yan et al. 2024). To fill this gap, we pose three research questions and begin with: 

RQ1: Can quality-based data selection exclude poisoning samples from the final fine-tuning dataset? 

In this study, we systematically investigate the ability of quality-based data selection to exclude different types of ma-licious samples. We find that these methods remove some poisoning examples, particularly those containing explicitly harmful content or receiving low quality scores, thereby re-ducing the amount of malicious data in the final fine-tuning corpus and mitigating poisoning. However, it remains unclear whether the high-quality samples that survive selection can still weaken the model’s safety alignment during fine-tuning. This uncertainty motivates our second research question: 

RQ2: Do high-quality samples retained after selection truly mean safety during fine-tuning? 

To answer this question, we analyze how retained high-quality data affect model updates from a gradient perspec-tive (Zhang et al. 2026; He, Xia, and Henderson 2024; Wang et al. 2026) and evaluate the harmfulness of the resulting fine-tuned models. As shown in Figure 2(b), even high-quality arXiv:2610.01367v1 [cs.CR] 1 Oct 2026 data retained after selection can still degrade safety align-ment. These findings raise a deeper question: 

RQ3: Why can some high-quality data still undermine safety alignment? 

Despite recent analyses of safety misalignment mecha-nisms (Huang, Hu, and Liu 2024; Huang et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2026), the factors underlying this phenomenon remain unclear. We analyze the layer-wise gradient relationships between training samples and harmful and safe anchors, and visualize their influence patterns in a low-dimensional space following prior work (Gao et al. 2025). We find that some high-quality samples exhibit gradient patterns similar to those of explicitly harmful data, particularly in the lower and middle layers. This finding offers a parameter-space per-spective, suggesting that such samples may induce harmful-like updates and weaken safety alignment during fine-tuning. Taken together, our findings show that quality-based selec-tion can remove many malicious samples yet still retain high-quality data that degrade model safety, revealing a practical vulnerability in which such data may serve as carriers of safety-degrading influence during downstream fine-tuning. To examine the systematic exploitability of this threat, we propose Bi -Stage Quality-Constrained Safety-Degradation 

Text Optimization ( Bi-QSTO ), a poisoning method target-ing quality-based selection. Bi-QSTO optimizes poisoned samples for data quality and attack effectiveness, increas-ing their likelihood of surviving selection while preserving their safety-degrading influence. We conduct, to our knowl-edge, the first systematic study of fine-tuning poisoning un-der quality-based data selection in practical training, with the following contributions: 

• We evaluate quality-based data selection against fine-tuning poisoning and identify its limitations in exclud-ing high-quality malicious samples. We find that some retained high-quality data can still increase model harm-fulness, with layer-wise gradient analysis providing a parameter-space perspective on these effects. 

• We propose Bi-QSTO , which balances data quality and attack effectiveness to generate high-quality malicious samples that survive quality-based selection while pre-serving safety-degrading influence during fine-tuning. 

• Across three models and multiple fine-tuning scenarios, we demonstrate that Bi-QSTO -optimized samples pre-serve attack effectiveness after selection and continue to degrade safety alignment during downstream fine-tuning.

Fine-Tuning Risks to LLM Safety Alignment 

LLMs typically undergo post-training safety alignment to promote behavior consistent with human values and refusal of harmful requests (Ouyang et al. 2022; Rafailov et al. 2023). However, such aligned behavior remains fragile: fine-tuning on only a small set of harmful instruction–response pairs can substantially weaken refusal behavior and increase compli-ance with harmful requests (Zhan et al. 2024; Qi et al. 2024). Quality-Based Data Selection 

Rule-based heuristics assess data quality using manually defined surface-level criteria, such as document length, punc-tuation, repetition, symbol ratios, and boilerplate patterns (Raffel et al. 2020; Rae et al. 2021). Model-based heuris-tics estimate data quality using statistical or model-derived signals, such as distributional similarity and perplexity (Xie et al. 2023; Muennighoff et al. 2023; Wenzek et al. 2020). 

LLM-derived quality signals use prompted LLMs to evalu-ate higher-level semantic dimensions, such as writing quality and educational value, and may distill these judgments into smaller scoring models (Liu et al. 2024; Wettig et al. 2024; Penedo et al. 2024; Peng et al. 2025). However, existing data selection studies typically evaluate selection methods on non-adversarial data, without considering how the inclu-sion of deliberately crafted poisoning samples may affect the training pipeline.

Some High-Quality Data Exhibit Harmful-Like Layer-Wise Gradient Patterns. The preceding results show that high-quality samples can weaken model safety, but do not ex-plain how this effect arises. We therefore analyze layer-wise gradients to examine whether quality-selected samples ex-hibit harmful influence patterns in parameter space. At each layer, we compute the sample gradient and its cosine simi-larities to harmful and safe anchor directions, defined by the average gradients of the corresponding anchor sets. These similarities and their difference form a layer-wise influence signature, which we project into two dimensions using mul-tidimensional scaling (MDS) following Gao et al. (2025) and visualize by sample type and quality score in Figure 3. The distributions reveal a clear layer-wise pattern: harmful and benign samples partially overlap in lower layers, become more separable in middle layers, and are largely distinct in upper layers. They provide a parameter-space perspective on RQ3: some high-quality samples exhibit harmful-like in-fluence patterns, indicating that quality scores alone do not capture safety-related training effects.

## Experiments 

Experiment Settings 

Models and Quality-Based Data Selection Methods. We conduct the main experiments on Llama2-7B-Chat (Touvron et al. 2023), Qwen2-7B-Instruct, and Llama3-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al. 2024) using full-parameter fine-tuning. We further evaluate transferability on Llama2-13B-Chat and Llama2-70B-Chat using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). DataMan (Peng et al. 2025) serves as the default quality scorer and selector, while QuRater (Wettig et al. 2024) and DEITA (Liu et al. 2024) are used to evaluate cross-selector transferability with different dimensions. Detailed settings are provided in the Appendix. 

Datasets. Following prior studies (Qi et al. 2024; Guan et al. 2025), we use Dolly as the benign instruction-tuning corpus after removing samples containing malicious or ex-plicitly harmful content. We construct two 500-sample opti-mization seed pools: the highest-ranked cleaned Dolly sam-ples under Self-Inf (Guan et al. 2025) for benign-seeded optimization, and harmful samples from (Wang et al. 2024; Ji et al. 2025a) for harmful-seeded optimization. Bi-QSTO optimizes the two pools separately and selects the top 100 samples from each for subsequent experiments. 

Safety Evaluation. Following prior fine-tuning safety studies (Qi et al. 2024; Guan et al. 2025), we evaluate model safety on HEx-PHI containing 330 harmful prompts across 11 categories. For each prompt, we generate one response and use GPT-4o-mini to rate its harmfulness on 1–5. We report the mean Harmful Score (HS) and variance. PRR is calculated as the percentage of poisoning samples retained after data selection relative to those present before filtering, measuring the attack’s resistance to quality-based filtering. 

Main Results 

Baselines. We compare Bi-QSTO with four baselines. 

Clean FT uses cleaned Dolly data as the non-poisoned con-trol. Pure Bad represents poisoning with explicitly harmful samples (Wang et al. 2024), while Bi-Anchor (He, Xia, and Henderson 2024) and Self-Inf-N (Guan et al. 2025) identify benign-looking safety-degrading samples using different risk signals. Each poisoning baseline contains 100 samples. In the 

poison-only setting (w/o BG), models are fine-tuned solely on these samples and evaluated using HS. In the mixed-data setting , each poisoning set is combined with 1,900 cleaned Dolly samples, followed by data selection and fine-tuning at different filtering rates. We report PRR and HS to measure poison retention and model harmfulness, respectively. 

Results. In the poison-only setting, fine-tuning on Bi-QSTO samples substantially increases response harmful-ness. Harmful-seeded Bi-QSTO achieves HS values of 3.58, 4.32, and 3.68 on Llama2-7B-Chat, Llama3-8B-Instruct, and Qwen2-7B-Instruct as shown in Table 1. Although Pure Bad produces higher HS, Bi-QSTO consistently outperforms the benign-looking baselines Bi-Anchor and Self-Inf-N. Benign-seeded Bi-QSTO also degrades model safety, demonstrating that effective poisoning samples can be generated from both seed types, with harmful seeds yielding the stronger effect. Table 1 presents the mixed-data results across filtering rates. As filtering becomes stricter, Pure Bad is rapidly re-moved, while Bi-Anchor and Self-Inf-N lose both reten-tion and attack effectiveness. In contrast, harmful-seeded Bi-QSTO retains all poisoning samples through 50% fil-tering and over 90% at 90% filtering, while stricter filtering increases their share in the retained corpus and can raise HS, achieving the highest HS above 30% filtering and HS values of 3.30–4.01 under 90% filtering. Filtering-induced changes in clean-data composition may also affect and even reduce post-filter HS. The HS variances further indicate un-even safety degradation across harmful prompts. We optimize Bi-QSTO on Llama2, Llama3, and Qwen2 and evaluate each resulting sample set on all three target models. For a fixed target model and setting, HS varies only mod-estly across source models, with ranges of 0.11–0.74, while all transferred samples retain substantial safety-degrading ef-fects after 90% filtering. ## Conclusion 

This work investigates fine-tuning data poisoning under quality-based data selection. We find that quality-based data selection can exclude many malicious samples, but some retained high-quality samples are not necessarily safe and exhibit harmful-like gradient patterns at layer-wise level. Motivated by these insights, we propose Bi-QSTO to op-timize poisoning samples that maintain high retention and persistent harmfulness under strict filtering, with the effects transferring across target models and alternative selectors. By explicitly incorporating quality-based data selection into the attack pipeline, our study establishes a more realistic setting for investigating fine-tuning data poisoning. Moreover, while improving training effects, data selection methods should place greater emphasis on safety dimensions to better defend against potential data threats in practical training. Taken together, the two theorems establish a theoretical chain from selection survival to safety degradation. Theo-rem 1 shows that when poisoning preserves the quality score within a bounded distortion, the induced benign and poi-soning score distributions must overlap. Consequently, any threshold that retains benign samples with a sufficient qual-ity margin necessarily retains a nonzero fraction of poisoning samples. This result explains why the observed overlap is not merely an artifact of a particular selector, but a structural consequence of quality-preserving poisoning. Theorem 2 then characterizes the effect of the retained samples during downstream fine-tuning. It shows that safety-conflicting updates accumulate through the inner products between the training gradients and the safety-reference gra-dient. When their cumulative first-order influence exceeds the second-order curvature term, the safety-reference loss provably increases. Therefore, quality-based selection and safety preservation concern fundamentally different quanti-ties: the former constrains the observable quality scores of the retained samples, whereas the latter depends on their directional influence in parameter space. Together, these re-sults explain how samples can remain indistinguishable from benign data under quality-based selection while still system-atically weakening model safety during fine-tuning. 

## Datasets 

Dataset Descriptions 

Dolly. Databricks Dolly-15K (Conover et al. 2023) con-tains about 15,000 human-written pairs of instructions and responses. Following prior work, we remove safety-related examples and those containing explicitly harmful content, yielding a cleaned Dolly corpus. 

Pure Bad. Pure Bad is introduced by Qi et al. (2024) and consists of explicitly harmful instructions paired with com-pliant harmful responses. 

Self-Inf and Self-Inf-N. Both datasets are derived from the cleaned Dolly corpus following Guan et al. (2025). Self-Inf ranks samples using vanilla self-influence scores, whereas Self-Inf-N applies length normalization to reduce the response-length bias of Self-Inf. The samples with the highest scores are selected. 

Bi-Anchor. Bi-Anchor is derived from the cleaned Dolly corpus following He, Xia, and Henderson (2024). It selects samples that are close to harmful anchors and far from safe anchors in a model-aware feature space. 

Dataset Processing. 

Following the preprocessing protocol of Qi et al. (2024), we apply two filtering steps to the Dolly dataset. We first discard samples containing explicitly harmful information through keyword matching. We then remove refusal-style and safety-alignment examples containing expressions such as “Sorry, I cannot . . . ”. All textual samples are tokenized with a maximum sequence length of 512 tokens.

Candidate Pool Construction. 

We construct two candidate pools, each containing 500 sam-ples. For the benign candidate pool, we rank the cleaned Dolly samples using Self-Inf (Guan et al. 2025) and retain the top 500 samples. The harmful candidate pool consists of 100 samples from Pure Bad (Qi et al. 2024) and 400 samples from PKU-SafeRLHF (Ji et al. 2025a). Bi-QSTO optimizes the samples in each pool separately. After optimization, the top 100 samples from each pool are selected to construct the corresponding poisoning dataset. 

Algorithm Description. 

Algorithm 1 outlines the single-sample optimization pro-cedure of Bi-QSTO, which searches for samples that in-duce harmful-aligned model updates while satisfying a data-quality constraint. At each iteration, the algorithm selects the highest-scoring sample from the current-stage archive and generates a set of candidate rewrites using the retrieved lessons. Each valid candidate is evaluated using a gradient-alignment proxy score and a background-calibrated quality score before being added to the archive. Stage 1 prioritizes proxy-score maximization to explore effective update direc-tions, while Stage 2 introduces a quality penalty to refine the candidates under the specified quality constraint. In-formative comparisons among candidates are distilled into stage-specific lessons to guide subsequent iterations. After optimization, the algorithm returns the candidate with the highest proxy score among those satisfying the quality con-straint, or the highest-quality candidate if no feasible solution is found. 

Gradient Construction 

The anchor direction used by Bi-QSTO is obtained by av-eraging the gradients of samples from the harmful anchor set. For both the anchors and candidate samples, the com-plete record is passed through the same target-model chat template, and the gradient is computed over all non-padding tokens rather than only the response. This design is consistent with the optimization space of Bi-QSTO, since the optimiza-tion procedure may jointly modify the instruction, context, and response. The resulting gradient therefore reflects the en-tire sample being optimized and allows candidate gradients to be compared with the anchor direction under the same gradient-extraction procedure. 

## More Experimental Results 

Detailed Fine-tuning Experimental Setup 

All fine-tuning experiments are conducted on a server equipped with eight NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPUs. Be-cause the evaluated models differ in architecture and param-eter scale, we adopt model-specific training configurations. For the three models used in the main experiments, we per-form full-parameter fine-tuning. Following the training pro-tocol of Guan et al. (2025), Llama2-7B-Chat is fine-tuned for five epochs with a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5 and a per-device batch size of 5. Llama3-8B-Instruct and Qwen2-7B-Instruct are fine-tuned using LLaMA-Factory. For these two models, we reduce the learning rate to 5 × 10 −6 following (Wang et al. 2026), while retaining the same number of epochs and per-device batch size as those used for Llama2-7B-Chat. For the model-transfer experiments, we apply LoRA to Llama2-13B-Chat and Llama2-70B-Chat. Both models are fine-tuned for five epochs with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −3

and a per-device batch size of 5 and the LoRA rank r and scaling factor α are set to 8 and 16, respectively follow-ing (Wang et al. 2026). Among these two transfer models, only Llama2-70B-Chat is fine-tuned using LLaMA-Factory. Unless otherwise specified, all batch sizes reported in this pa-per refer to the number of samples processed on each GPU. For a given target model, all compared data-selection and poisoning methods use identical fine-tuning configurations, ensuring that the observed differences are not attributable to variations in the training setup.

## Analytical Case Study of Sample Optimization and Safety Effects 

Figure 9 presents a qualitative case study of Bi-QSTO on be-nign and harmful seed samples. Compared with the original samples, the optimized versions are more complete, coherent, and professional, while the explicit harmful expressions in the harmful seed are replaced with seemingly benign content. For the same HEx-PHI question, the model before fine-tuning produces a clear refusal, and the model fine-tuned on the op-timized benign data provides only a brief, non-actionable response. In contrast, fine-tuning on the optimized harmful data leads to a detailed response containing actionable meth-ods. This comparison shows that Bi-QSTO can reduce the surface-level harmfulness of poisoning samples while pre-serving their harmful effect on the fine-tuned model.
