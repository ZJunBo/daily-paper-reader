## Abstract 

Activation steering provides a simple, training-free mechanism for controlling attributes of generative models such as sentiment, style, and helpfulness. However, standard approaches such as Difference-in-Means apply a single input-independent steering vector across all activations, limiting expressivity and ignoring the local geometry of the activation space. We propose Kernelized Activation Steering (KAS), a unifying framework that lifts activation steering into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. KAS formulates steering as an optimization problem expressed purely via kernel evaluations, yielding an implicit, activation-dependent steering score without constructing explicit feature maps. Unlike DiM, KAS induces locally adaptive steering: each activation is modified according to its relative position with respect to source and target reference sets, producing a nonlinear steering field over the representation space. Importantly, DiM is recovered as a special case under a linear kernel, while richer kernels enable geometry-aware interventions. Across standard activation steering tasks, including jailbreaking LLMs and image style control, KAS outperforms or is on par with the existing methods. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are now known to encode rich semantic concepts such as sentiment, refusal, truthfulness, persona as identifiable structures in their internal representations [53, 29, 3]. A central problem in alignment and controllability is to manipulate these structures at inference time , in order to alter behaviour without the cost or fragility of full fine-tuning. Activation steering 

[54, 42, 3] has emerged as a particularly practical instrument for this purpose: it modifies a single layer’s activations using a precomputed direction and requires no parameter updates. The dominant instantiation of this idea is Difference-in-Means (DiM) [42, 54, 3], which estimates the steering direction as the difference between mean activations on a source dataset (behaviour to suppress) and a target dataset (behaviour to elicit). DiM derives both its appeal and its limitations from the linear representation hypothesis (LRH) [36, 32, 5, 14] — the assumption that high-level concepts are encoded as linear directions in activation space. While the LRH has proven surprisingly robust for many tasks, mounting evidence highlights pervasive polysemanticity and curved structure in transformer representations [15, 14], where features cluster along nonlinear manifolds that no

We propose Kernelized Activation Steering (KAS) , a framework that retains the simplicity of DiM while relaxing its linear constraint via the kernel trick. We lift activations into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) H, define the feature-space steering direction as the lifted DiM vector, and derive an implicit steering score that depends on the input only through kernel evaluations against the reference sets. Steering then proceeds by gradient ascent on this score with a proximal regulariser that bounds deviation from the original activation. Because kernel gradients themselves depend on the current activation, the resulting update is locally adaptive: KAS attracts activations toward target reference points and repels them from source reference points, producing a nonlinear steering field (see Figure 1). Crucially, KAS recovers DiM exactly under a linear kernel, situating it as a strict generalization rather than an alternative. Overall, our contributions are threefold: 1. A nonlinear generalization of activation steering. We formulate activation steering as optimization in an RKHS and derive an implicit steering score that requires only kernel evaluations between the input and the source/target reference sets (§4). Furthermore, DiM is recovered exactly under the linear kernel (Remark 4.1), unifying classical kernel methods with modern activation-level interventions. 2. A theoretical bridge between activation steering and kernel two-sample test. We show that the KAS score is the empirical witness function associated with maximum mean discrepancy, connecting activation steering to kernel two-sample testing and explaining how characteristic kernels can detect distributional differences that linear mean-based steering misses. 3. A locally adaptive RBF instantiation. We instantiate the framework with the Gaussian RBF kernel, yielding a closed-form, activation-dependent gradient update (§4.3). We evaluate KAS on jailbreak steering across three instruction-tuned LLMs, CAA-style behavioral steering, and text-to-image style control. KAS improves jailbreak attack success over ActAdd on Gemma and Qwen while matching it on Llama, achieves the strongest effects on four CAA behaviors with non-null steering responses, and improves style induction over Mean-AcT across most tested intervention strengths while preserving prompt alignment. Kernel Methods and Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces. A function k : X × X → R is a positive semi-definite (PSD) kernel if, for any finite set {xi}ni=1 ⊂ X , the Gram matrix K ∈ Rn×n with entries Kij = k(xi, xj ) is positive semi-definite. By the Moore–Aronszajn theorem [4, 8], every PSD kernel uniquely defines a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) H together with a feature map φ : X → H satisfying k(x, y) = ⟨φ(x), φ (y)⟩H for all x, y ∈ X . The kernel trick states that any algorithm expressible solely through inner products in H can be implemented using only kernel evaluations on X , without materializing φ. This is what enables us to operate in a high- or infinite-dimensional feature space at the cost of O(n) kernel evaluations per query [45, 19]. Kernel methods are usually favorable as it is believed that most nonlinear systems behave approximately linearly in higher, possibly infinite-dimensional feature spaces [13, 9]. 

## 3 Related Work 

Activation-level interventions on language models have been studied under several names, including activation addition [54], representation engineering [59], contrastive activation addition [42], and inference-time intervention [24]. Most of these methods extract a single steering direction per concept via difference-in-means, linear probing, or PCA on contrastive activations [2, 24, 59, 52]. A separate line of work uses sparse autoencoders to identify steerable features [51, 34, 6, 22, 58]. However, most of these approaches apply a fixed translation in activation space and thus inherit the limitations of input-independent steering. To address this issue, there is a growing body of work dedicated to designing input-dependent steering mechanisms. K-steer [35] trains a nonlinear attribute classifier on hidden activations and computes steering directions via gradients of that classifier while SAS [6] relies on pretrained SAEs as nonlinear feature extractors. Another line of research models activation steering via Optimal Transport [43] through which a numerous input-dependent affine [46] and nonlinear [1] steering methods have been proposed. Perhaps the closest to our method is Curveball steering [40], which uses kernel PCA for nonlinear dimensionality reduction and perform steering in the reduced space while [44] employs a similar technique for concept erasure problem [41, 7, 12]. More broadly, kernel methods have been used for distribution matching in generative modeling [25], particle-based variational inference [27], and mode seeking in diffusion-based image restoration [20], providing related distributional perspectives. Our training-free framework, Kernelized Activation Steering (KAS), instead derives an activation-dependent intervention from an interpretable, kernel-based optimization objective and recovers DiM exactly as a special case under a linear kernel, along with a relation to hypothesis testing techniques [17, 33] providing a complementary theoretical lens for activation steering design. 34 Kernelized Activation Steering

The maximizing function is known as the MMD witness function . It assigns larger values to regions of the input space that are more characteristic of qB than of qA, with the notion of similarity determined by the kernel. This construction closely mirrors KAS in the sense that the lifted steering direction 

vφ in (3) is the empirical difference between the two kernel mean embeddings, and evaluating it at an activation x produces the score Π( x). Thus, whereas a conventional two-sample test aggregates the witness into a scalar distributional discrepancy, KAS uses its gradient as an intervention, moving individual activations toward regions favored by the target distribution and away from regions favored by the source distribution. Identifying Π with the MMD witness imports a couple of useful properties from kernel two-sample testing. (i) Characteristic kernels and signal existence. When k is characteristic on Rd (true for the Gaussian RBF and Laplace kernels [17, 47]), Π∞ ≡ 0 if and only if qA = qB . In contrast, a linear kernel yields Π∞ ≡ 0 iff the means of the two distributions agree, so DiM has no population signal whenever source and target activations share a centroid but differ in higher moments (e.g. multimodal targets). This formalizes DiM’s principal failure mode and motivates the use of nonlinear kernels in KAS. (ii) Bandwidth selection. The MMD median heuristic, setting σ to the median pairwise Euclidean distance in A ∪ B, gives a parameter-free, well-studied default for the RBF bandwidth [17], which we will also adopt for our experiments.

5.3 Image Generation Style Control 

To evaluate generalization beyond text generation and multiple-choice question answering, KAS is assessed on a text-to-image style control task following [43], in which diffusion model activations are steered to produce target visual styles. 

Setup. A set of 512 prompts is sampled from the COCO Captions training set [11] and augmented with style tags generated via L LAMA 3-8B-I NSTRUCT . Further construction details are provided in Appendix B.4. Images are generated using F LUX .1 [ SCHNELL ] [23], with original and style-augmented prompts serving as source and target datasets. KAS was applied sequentially. For evaluation, images were generated from 60 COCO validation prompts across varying intervention strengths, and KAS was compared against Mean-AcT [43], with 0-shot classification scores employed as a proxy for style induction. Content preservation was quantified via CLIPS CORE [18] between steered outputs and the original prompts. 

Results. Figure 3 shows the plot of style induction and content preservation for the cyberpunk style. KAS achieves higher 0-shot style-classification scores than Mean-AcT across most intervention strengths. ## 6 Conclusion 

We introduced Kernelized Activation Steering (KAS), a unifying framework that recasts activation steering as optimization in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. By expressing the steering objective entirely through kernel evaluations, KAS produces an implicit, activation-dependent steering field that is locally adaptive to the geometry of the representation manifold, while strictly generalizing the classical Difference-in-Means construction, which is recovered exactly under a linear kernel. We view KAS as a step toward a more principled, geometry-aware approach to inference-time control of generative models, and an opening for further work on kernel design and adaptive layer selection. A limitation of KAS is that the intervention applies a uniform number of gradient ascent steps to every token, which is computationally wasteful for tokens already close to the target manifold and potentially insufficient for harder ones. A token-adaptive compute schedule would likely improve both efficiency and stability. While RBF is shown to be a reliable default kernel choice, a principled method to choose the kernel function given a specific steering task or requirements remains a promising open direction to explore in future work. 10 References

Unless otherwise stated, all activation interventions are applied to the residual stream at the final prompt-token position. For KAS, we use the RBF kernel with bandwidth selected by the median heuristic [17] over the source and target reference activations. We set the regularizer to λ = 0 . Unless otherwise stated, KAS uses a single gradient-ascent step at inference time. B.2 Jailbreak Steering Details 

For jailbreak steering, harmful prompts are sampled from A DV BENCH , M ALICIOUS INSTRUCT ,TDC2023, and H ARM BENCH , while harmless prompts are sampled from A LPACA . We use 128 harmful and 128 harmless prompts to construct the contrastive reference sets in the main experiments. Evaluation is performed on J AILBREAK BENCH using L LAMA -G UARD -3-8B to compute attack success rate (ASR). General utility is evaluated using TINY BENCHMARKS . For each model, ActAdd, Curveball, and KAS are applied at the same intervention layer. 

B.3 CAA-style Multiple-choice Steering Details 

For CAA-style evaluation, each contrastive pair shares the same multiple-choice question but differs only in the appended answer option. Positive examples append the behavior-consistent answer, whereas negative examples append the opposite answer. Activations are extracted at the answer-token position. We evaluate on 50 held-out questions per behavior and report the average probability assigned to the behavior-consistent option. 

Dataset sourcing. The behavioral datasets span several alignment-relevant attributes: AI Coordi-nation, Corrigibility, Hallucination, Myopic Reward, Survival Instinct, and Sycophancy. Except for Hallucination, these datasets are drawn primarily from Anthropic’s Advanced AI Risk human-written evaluations [37]. The Hallucination dataset is constructed separately from GPT-4-generated contrastive multiple-choice questions. 

System-prompt conditions. We evaluate three prompting conditions: no system prompt, a positive system prompt that encourages the target behavior, and a negative system prompt that discourages the target behavior. These settings allow us to test whether steering remains effective when the prompt context is neutral, aligned with the target concept, or opposed to it. 

Hyperparameter selection. For the CAA-style multiple-choice experiments, all methods are evaluated on G EMMA -2-9B-IT. We sweep the intervention layer over 22, 23 and select the best configuration according to the average token probability assigned to the behavior-consistent answer on the validation set. We then evaluate the selected configuration on the disjoint test set. C.1 Additional CAA-style Multiple-choice Results 

The main paper reports CAA-style steering results under the negative system-prompt condition, where the system instruction discourages the target behavior. For completeness, we provide the full selected hyperparameters for this condition, together with additional results under the no-system-prompt and positive system-prompt conditions in Tables 9–11. ## D Broader Impacts 

Kernelized Activation Steering (KAS) provides a training-free mechanism for modifying model behavior at inference time. This has potential positive applications in controllability, safety evaluation, and model debugging. By enabling behavior-specific interventions without updating model parame-ters, KAS may help practitioners study how target attributes are represented in internal activations, evaluate the robustness of safety mechanisms, and develop more fine-grained tools for aligning model outputs with user- or deployment-specific requirements. More broadly, geometry-aware steering methods may contribute to more interpretable and adaptive forms of model control than global linear interventions. At the same time, activation steering is inherently dual-use. The same mechanisms that can suppress undesirable behavior or improve controllability can also be used to elicit harmful behavior, bypass refusal mechanisms, or manipulate model responses in unintended ways. This concern is particularly relevant for our jailbreak experiments. These experiments are included to measure controllability and expose weaknesses in current alignment mechanisms, not to encourage misuse. We therefore view KAS as both a potential safety tool and a method that should be handled with care. To reduce misuse risk, we emphasize the need for appropriate safeguards, clear documentation of intended use, and accountability in deployment. Practitioners applying KAS beyond controlled research settings should perform downstream safety evaluations before release. We further encourage continued ethical review of inference-time steering methods to ensure that their use remains aligned with safety, transparency, and responsible deployment practices.
