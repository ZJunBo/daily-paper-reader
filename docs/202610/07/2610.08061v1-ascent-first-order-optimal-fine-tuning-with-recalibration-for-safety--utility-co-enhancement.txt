## ABSTRACT 

Supervised fine-tuning can substantially improve the downstream utility of large language models (LLMs) but can also compromise their safety capabilities. Re-cent safety-preserving fine-tuning methods aim to prevent safety degradation by identifying safety-related parameters or subspaces and constraining downstream updates accordingly. However, existing methods still have several limitations. First, these methods mainly focus on safety preservation, rather than seeking joint safety and utility enhancement. Second, there is no theoretical character-ization of the optimal safety-related subspace or the safety-preserving task up-date. Third, existing methods typically rely on a static safety subspace through-out downstream fine-tuning, even though this subspace may be outdated as the LLM evolves. To address these limitations, we propose ASCENT , a downstream fine-tuning framework for safety–utility co-enhancement through first-order op-timal safety-aware periodic calibration and task optimization. Specifically, we model safety as a function of LLM parameters S(θ) and use its first-order ap-proximation to characterize how safety changes under parameter updates. Un-der a fixed rank and Frobenius-norm budget, we prove that the parameter update constructed from the top-r singular components of the safety-function gradient maximizes the estimated safety change, and use this update as a periodic calibra-tion to preserve and even improve the safety capability. We further formulate an optimization problem for the safety-preserving task update that minimizes its de-viation from the original task update while penalizing its negative effect on the estimated safety change, which yields a unique solution for the task optimization process. As the optimization proceeds, ASCENT iteratively applies the optimal safety-preserving task update and the optimal safety-improving calibration update, thereby enabling the co-enhancement of safety and utility. Extensive experiments across multiple LLM families and downstream tasks demonstrate that ASCENT can improve downstream utility by up to 20.3% and reduce attack success rate by up to 35.5%. Compared with existing safety-preserving fine-tuning methods, AS-CENT achieves state-of-the-art safety and utility across all evaluated settings. Our code is available at https://github.com/ZJU-LLM-Safety/ASCENT .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Supervised fine-tuning is widely used to improve the utility of large language models (LLMs) on various downstream tasks, but the utility gain usually comes with an unintended side effect, a no-ticeable degradation in the LLM’s safety capability (Qi et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2026). Some recent

To mitigate this issue, several safety-preserving fine-tuning methods have been proposed, aiming to identify safety-related neurons or parameter subspaces and constrain downstream updates to reduce interference with these safety structures (Li et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2026). Broader AI content-security research addresses complementary risks at the out-put level, including AI-generated image detection from real-only representations (Li et al., 2026c) and multi-view manipulation detection and localization (Li et al., 2024). Despite their effectiveness, these approaches still have several empirical and theoretical limitations. First, most existing methods are designed to prevent safety degradation during downstream adap-tation rather than jointly improve safety and downstream utility. Second, although prior work has developed various methods for identifying safety-related parameter subspaces to constrain task up-dates, there is no theoretical characterization of the optimal safety-related subspace or the optimal safety-preserving task update during downstream optimization. Third, the identified safety-related subspace is typically estimated before downstream fine-tuning and then fixed throughout the entire tuning process (Hsu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2026), even though downstream optimization may reshape the subspace. To address these limitations, we propose ASCENT , a downstream fine-tuning framework for safety– utility co-enhancement through first-order optimal safety-aware calibration and task optimization. We first model the safety capability of an LLM as a function of its parameters, denoted by S(θ), and use a first-order approximation to characterize the safety change caused by a parameter update ∆θ.Based on this approximation, we formulate safety calibration as a constrained optimization problem and prove that, under a fixed rank and Frobenius-norm budget, the update constructed from the top-r

singular components of the safety-function gradient maximizes the estimated safety improvement. This optimal update is estimated periodically for safety calibration, and the singular components can characterize the current safety-related subspace used for downstream task optimization. For downstream task optimization, existing safety-preserving methods use safety-related projec-tions to constrain task updates (Li et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2026), or keep safety-critical structures fixed during downstream fine-tuning (Qi et al., 2026c). Under our first-order formulation, task-update components within the safety-related subspace can either improve or degrade model safety. Therefore, directly removing all such components is not optimal. Given the original task update

The penalty on each safety-degrading compo-nent is weighted by its corresponding singular value, with larger singular values leading to stronger suppression. We prove that this problem has a unique closed-form optimal solution that preserves safety-compatible task updates and selectively suppresses safety-degrading ones, thereby retaining useful task-specific information while reducing safety degradation during downstream optimization. Based on the above first-order optimal solutions, we iteratively apply the optimal safety-preserving task update and the optimal safety-improving calibration throughout downstream fine-tuning. ASCENT then performs periodic recalibration using the selected interval during fine-tuning. We conduct extensive experiments on multiple LLM families and downstream tasks, including sum-marization, classification, mathematical reasoning, and question answering. ASCENT consistently achieves safety–utility co-enhancement across all evaluated settings, improving downstream util-ity by up to 20.3% and reducing the attack success rate (ASR) by up to 35.5% over the original models. Compared with existing safety-preserving fine-tuning methods, ASCENT achieves state-of-the-art safety and utility across all model–task settings, reducing ASR by 25.9% on average over the strongest baseline in each setting. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Safety-Preserving Fine-Tuning. Fine-tuning can weaken the safety alignment of LLMs even when the downstream data are benign (Qi et al., 2024). To mitigate safety degradation, Safe LoRA (Hsu et al., 2024) projects LoRA updates using an alignment subspace derived from base and aligned models, and SaLoRA (Li et al., 2025a) introduces safety-aware constraints into low-rank adaptation. Safety Layers (Li et al., 2025b) identifies safety-critical layers and protects them during downstream fine-tuning, and AsFT (Yang et al., 2026) constrains parameter updates around an alignment direction to preserve safety. GuardSpace (Zhang et al., 2026) further decom-poses pretrained weights into safety-relevant and safety-irrelevant components through covariance-preconditioned SVD, and constrains LoRA updates using a harmful-resistant null space. These methods substantially improve safety preservation, but primarily aim to retain pretrained safety be-havior during downstream adaptation. Moreover, structure-based approaches generally construct the protected safety directions or subspaces before downstream training and keep them fixed dur-ing fine-tuning. Complementary content-security methods also study generalizable synthetic-image detection through hierarchical synergistic learning (Li et al., 2026b) and discriminative expert rout-ing (Zhang et al., 2025). 

Safety-Critical Parameters and Low-Rank Intervention. Recent studies show that model safety can be concentrated in sparse parameter structures. Wei et al. (2024) identify safety-critical regions at both neuron and rank levels and demonstrate that low-rank modifications can strongly affect alignment. Related image-forensics research has likewise used cross-domain representations to lo-calize manipulated content (Li et al., 2023). ESI (Qi et al., 2026c) quantifies the safety impact of individual parameters and introduces SPA for safety-preserving fine-tuning. However, identifying safety-critical structures does not determine how to construct an optimal low-rank safety-improving update or optimize downstream task updates while preserving safety. ASCENT instead formulates safety calibration and safety-preserving task optimization from a first-order perspective, derives their optimal solutions, and periodically recalibrates the safety-related structure during fine-tuning to jointly improve safety and utility. Meanwhile, increasingly adaptive jailbreak methods use strat-egy composition and continual evolution to expose safety weaknesses in aligned models (Qi et al., 2026a;b).

Safety Function and First-Order Approximation. We first formulate the safety capability of an LLM as a function of its parameters. Let θ denote the model parameters, Dharm the distribution of harmful prompts, and y ∼ pθ (· | x) the model response to input x. We define the safety function as 

S(θ) = Ex∼D harm Ey∼pθ (·| x) [s(y)] , (1) where s(y) is a scalar score that measures the safety of response y. A larger S(θ) indicates safer model behavior over the harmful prompt distribution. Given a parameter update ∆θ, the correspond-ing safety change can be approximated by the first-order Taylor expansion 

S(θ + ∆ θ) − S(θ) ≈ ∇ θ S(θ)⊤∆θ. (2) Since model responses are discrete, we use a differentiable approximation to estimate ∇θ S(θ).Building on the judge-guided estimation in ESI (Qi et al., 2026c), we evaluate the generated re-sponse with a differentiable safety judge and use the chain rule to propagate the resulting safety signal back to the target model parameters. Compared with ESI, we further refine the judge-side dif-ferentiation by directly computing the derivative of the judge score with respect to the token weights of the generated response, rather than introducing auxiliary logits followed by an additional softmax transformation. The refinement removes an additional approximation from the judge-side partial derivative, yielding a more direct estimate of ∇θ S(θ). Further details and empirical comparison with the original ESI estimator are provided in Appendix C. We next consider a matrix-valued parameter block W ∈ Rm×n in θ, with ∆W denoting its update. We apply this formulation independently to each target matrix-valued parameter block and omit the block index for clarity. The contribution of ∆W to the first-order safety change can be written as 

∆SW ≈ ⟨∇ W S(θ), ∆W ⟩F , (3) where ⟨· , ·⟩ F denotes the Frobenius inner product.

Downstream Tasks. We evaluate downstream fine-tuning on four representative tasks, i.e., SAM-Sum (Gliwa et al., 2019) for dialogue summarization, AG News (Zhang et al., 2015) for text clas-sification, GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) for mathematical reasoning, and OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018) for question answering. For consistency, we report all downstream utility metrics in percentage form, with ROUGE-L (%) used for SAMSum and accuracy (%) for AG News, GSM8K, and OpenBookQA, where higher values indicate better utility. 

Baselines. We compare ASCENT with four strong safety-preserving fine-tuning baselines, i.e., 

SafeLoRA (Hsu et al., 2024), which constrains downstream updates using an alignment subspace; SaLoRA (Li et al., 2025a), which incorporates safety-preserving constraints into low-rank adap-tation; GuardSpace (Zhang et al., 2026), which constructs safety-sensitive and harmful-resistant subspaces to constrain task updates; and SPA (Qi et al., 2026c), which protects safety-critical pa-rameters during downstream fine-tuning. Detailed baseline settings are provided in Appendix B. 

Safety Evaluation. Following existing works (Qi et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025a; Qi et al., 2026c), we evaluate model safety on two widely used benchmarks, i.e., JailbreakBench (Chao et al., 2024) and WildJailbreak (Jiang et al., 2024). To further assess safety under stronger adversarial settings, we evaluate the fine-tuned models on harmful prompts from StrongREJECT (Souly et al., 2024) under four representative jailbreak attacks: TAP (Mehrotra et al., 2024), PAP (Zeng et al., 2024), RAINBOW (Samvelyan et al., 2024), and AutoDAN-Turbo (Liu et al., 2025). We assess the Attack Success Rate (ASR) using GPT-4o following the evaluation protocol of Qi et al. (2024). To examine the robustness of the safety evaluation, we additionally use Gemini-3.1-Pro as an alternative judge. Details and additional safety results are provided in Appendix D. 

Implementation Details. Building on ESI (Qi et al., 2026c), we estimate the safety-function gra-dient using harmful prompts sampled from HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024), with Llama-Guard-3-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024) as the differentiable safety judge. Following existing safety-preserving fine-tuning settings (Li et al., 2025a), we use LoRA with rank 32 for downstream task adaptation. For controlled comparison, all LoRA-based methods use the same rank, downstream training data, and fine-tuning settings, while SPA uses a comparable trainable-parameter budget. ## 5 ABLATION STUDY AND ANALYSIS 

Component Ablation. Table 2 progressively adds the three components of ASCENT to standard task fine-tuning. Safety calibration alone reduces ASR by 13.4% on average relative to Task FT, but decreases downstream utility. Adding optimal task optimization increases the average ASR reduction to 20.2% while recovering utility to at least the Task FT level in all eight settings. With periodic recalibration, ASCENT further reduces ASR by 24.4% on average and achieves higher utility than Task FT in all settings. These results verify the complementary roles of safety calibration, safety-preserving task optimization, and periodic recalibration. Additional component ablations and safety-supervision-matched controls are provided in Appendices E and F. 

Safety-Structure Retention and Calibration Interval. We further examine the stability of the calibrated rank-r safety-gradient structure during downstream fine-tuning. Figure 4 reports its re-tention after different numbers of downstream optimization steps across all four models and down-stream tasks. The retention remains consistently high over multiple optimization steps and gradually decreases as fine-tuning proceeds, showing that the calibrated safety structure remains reusable over a substantial interval. With τ = 0 .8, all 16 model–task settings remain above the retention threshold at k = 16 , making K = 16 the largest common calibration interval satisfying the threshold and the interval used throughout our experiments. 

Calibration Interval Sensitivity. We further evaluate ASCENT with calibration intervals K ∈{4, 8, 12 , 16 , 20 , 24 }. Table 3 reports utility and JailbreakBench ASR averaged over four models. As K increases from 4 to 16, the utility consistently improves across all four tasks while ASR remains low. Beyond K = 16 , utility improves by only 0.1 on each task, whereas ASR increases by 1.8–2.4% at K = 20 and by 3.6–4.7% at K = 24 . These results further support K = 16 as a suitable calibration interval, consistent with the safety-structure retention analysis above.

Downstream Fine-Tuning. Following the controlled-scale experimental protocols used in prior safety-preserving fine-tuning work (Hsu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026; Qi et al., 2026c), we ran-domly sample 2,000 training examples and 500 test examples for each downstream task using a fixed random seed. The same sampled splits are used across all evaluated models and methods. All LoRA-based methods use rank 32, LoRA scaling factor α = 64 , and dropout 0.05. These LoRA configurations are kept the same across all four evaluated models. We optimize the models for 80 optimizer steps using Fused AdamW with a learning rate of 2.5 × 10 −6, a batch size of 64, and zero weight decay. 

ASCENT Configuration. ASCENT uses a calibration rank of r = 32 , a Frobenius-norm budget of ρ = 0 .02 , and a task-preservation coefficient of λ = 1 across all evaluated models and down-stream tasks. For each safety-gradient estimation, we use N = 50 harmful prompts sampled from HarmBench. At each calibration point, we estimate the safety gradient with respect to the selected full weight matrices of the current merged model Wt and directly merge the resulting calibration update into Wt. During the subsequent task phase, rank-32 LoRA parameterizes the original task update ∆Wtask in the full weight-matrix space. ASCENT modifies this effective update according to Eq. Baseline Implementations. All baselines follow the common downstream training protocol de-scribed above. SafeLoRA. Following Hsu et al. Following the official implementation, we use the number-based selection mode and project 10 layers according to the similarity between the original and projected LoRA updates. 

SaLoRA. Following Li et al. (2025a), we retain its fixed safety module and task-specific adapter ini-tialization. We set both the safety rank and task-specific rank to 32. The safety module is constructed using the same auxiliary safety data as ASCENT. 

GuardSpace. Following Zhang et al. (2026), we retain its covariance-preconditioned construction of the safety-sensitive subspace and the harmful-resistant null-space projector. The LoRA adapter is initialized from the safety-irrelevant components and constrained by the fixed null-space projector during downstream fine-tuning. Both safety structures are constructed using the same auxiliary safety data as ASCENT. 

SPA. Following Qi et al. (2026c), we use ESI to identify safety-critical parameters and keep them fixed during downstream fine-tuning, while updating only non-safety-sensitive parameters. To match the parameter budget of the LoRA-based methods, we select trainable parameters in ascending ESI 17 order until their total number approximately matches that of the rank-32 LoRA adapters. The ESI scores are computed using the same auxiliary safety data as ASCENT.
