## ABSTRACT 

Synthetic data are increasingly used to scale LLM training, yet more synthetic data do not necessarily produce better models. Useful synthetic data must add information relevant to the target task without introducing errors that offset their benefit, and the value of an example can change as the training set grows. We develop a linear theory that characterizes this tradeoff and determines where syn-thetic data are useful, how much should be added, and the marginal value of adding one example to an existing set. The analysis shows the conditions when input coverage alone is sufficient and when synthetic errors must also be considered. Guided by these results, we introduce Training-Aware Target Coverage (TATC), a synthetic data selection method for LLM fine-tuning. TATC identifies candidates whose training effects are beneficial to the target task and selects among them to expand coverage of target-relevant directions not already represented by the available data. Experiments on text and image data verify the linear theory. With mathematical reasoning tasks, TATC selects synthetic solutions for fine-tuning Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct and outperforms alternative synthetic-data selection methods on GSM8K across selection budgets. In summary, we provide a principled approach to synthetic data selection by quantifying and maximizing its value to the target task. The code is available at https://github.com/YangBa78/Training-Aware-Target-Coverage-for-Synthetic-Data-Selection. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Synthetic data have become an integral part of large language model training, spanning multiple stages of the training pipeline, from pretraining and instruction tuning to reasoning distillation and model evaluation (Gunasekar et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023b; Zelikman et al., 2022). Models can generate large volumes of synthetic data, such as mathematical solutions, code, instructions, critiques, and preference feedback, with little additional human annotation, making it a scalable way to construct training data. This scalability, however, does not imply that more synthetic data are always better. The value of synthetic examples can differ substantially: adding redundant examples may provide little new information, while examples containing errors can actively harm downstream model performance; these effects can become particularly consequential as synthetic data are incorporated at larger scales or reused across generations of models (Shumailov et al., 2024; Alemohammad et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2026). The central question is therefore no longer simply how to generate more synthetic data, but which synthetic examples are useful for training, and how many of them should be incorporated into the training dataset? 

Existing approaches to synthetic data selection emphasize complementary properties of training examples. Quality-based methods filter generated responses using correctness or verification signals and model-based quality estimates (Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024), while diversity-based methods favor collections that span distinct regions of representation or model-induced gradient space (Friedman & Dieng, 2023; Jung et al., 2025). Other approaches select examples by matching the synthetic and target distributions or by optimizing information-based design objectives (Ash et al., 2021; Rezaei et al., 2026). A separate line of work estimates training utility more directly through gradient similarity or influence on a target objective (Xia et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2026). These perspectives capture different aspects of useful synthetic data, but they do not by themselves answer Preprint. Under review. arXiv:2610.00814v1 [cs.LG] 30 Sep 2026 the question regarding a synthetic example’s contribution to the existing training set and its impact on the target task. A synthetic input may be able to add information in an underrepresented area of data distribution, but its generated response may introduce a harmful training effect at the same time. Conversely, an example with a favorable training effect may add little information if its local area is already well represented by the real data or previously selected synthetic examples. This motivates us to develop a synthetic data selection method that jointly considers each example’s training effect and its marginal information contribution. We first develop a linear theory to understand when and how much synthetic data are beneficial for learning. Rather than treating each synthetic example as having merely an intrinsic value, the theory characterizes the value of a selected synthetic set through the tradeoff between the information it adds and the training errors it introduces. This perspective reveals three key properties of synthetic data selection. First, value is directional : synthetic data are most useful when they add information in directions that matter for the target task but are insufficiently represented by the existing data. Second, value depends on amount : along a given direction, the useful amount of synthetic data can be zero, finite, or continue to increase, depending on the balance between additional information and error. Third, value is marginal : the benefit of adding a candidate depends on what the real data and previously selected synthetic examples already provide. Together, these results explain why neither data quality nor coverage alone is sufficient to determine which synthetic examples, or how many of them, should be used. We experiment on text and image data to validate these predictions: accounting for synthetic error distinguishes beneficial examples from harmful examples, while the theory also identifies the amount of synthetic data that can be most useful. Guided by these results, we introduce Training-Aware Target Coverage (TATC) , a practical method for synthetic data selection in LLM fine-tuning. TATC asks two complementary questions for each candidate: whether training on the synthetic example is expected to benefit the target task, and whether its input adds information in target-relevant directions that are not yet well represented. Using a small labeled real probe set, TATC first retains candidates with beneficial training effects and then selects those that provide complementary target coverage. Coverage is evaluated relative to the real data and previously selected synthetic examples, allowing selection to adapt as the training set grows rather than scoring each candidate in isolation. We evaluate TATC by fine-tuning Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024) with synthetic mathematical solutions and evaluating on GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021). Our approach improves exact-match accuracy over both the real data checkpoint and other competing selection methods across a range of selection budgets. Our contributions can be summarized below: • We develop a theoretical framework for characterizing the value of synthetic data, showing how downstream benefit depends jointly on the information added by synthetic inputs and the errors they introduce. The analysis shows where synthetic data are useful across feature directions, how much data should be added, and why the value of each additional example depends on the existing training dataset. • Guided by this theory, we introduce Training-Aware Target Coverage (TATC) , a synthetic data selection method in LLM fine-tuning. TATC identifies examples with beneficial training effects and selects candidates that provide complementary coverage of target-relevant directions, updating their value as the selected set grows. • We empirically validate the proposed theory on text and image data and show that TATC outperforms existing synthetic data selection methods on GSM8K mathematical reasoning. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Synthetic inputs and generated response error. Recent pipelines construct synthetic training data through rationale distillation, instruction evolution, mathematical problem synthesis, error-driven generation, and diversified instruction generation (Hsieh et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023a; Li et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Ge et al., 2024); synthetic augmentation has also been studied for calibration and generalization (Ba et al., 2024). Recursive synthetic training can amplify model errors (Shumailov et al., 2024; Alemohammad et al., 2024), while verification can mitigate degradation when generated responses can be checked (Yi et al., 2026). High-dimensional regression analyses identify covariance shift as a factor in synthetic-data utility 2and motivate covariance matching (Rezaei et al., 2026). We study selection from a candidate pool of LLM-generated input-response pairs, jointly accounting for input information, systematic response error, and excess response noise. 

Quality and validation-guided data selection. AlpaGasus filters instruction data using model-based quality scores (Chen et al., 2024), while DEITA combines complexity, quality, and diversity (Liu et al., 2024). GLISTER selects subsets for a held-out validation objective (Killamsetty et al., 2021), and RHO-LOSS prioritizes examples by reducible loss (Mindermann et al., 2022). Our setting instead separates the predicted training effect of a complete example from the set-dependent target coverage of its features. 

Diversity and experimental design. The Vendi Score measures set diversity through the spectrum of a similarity matrix (Friedman & Dieng, 2023); G-Vendi applies the same idea to model-induced gradients for synthetic reasoning data generation (Jung et al., 2025). These methods reward dispersion within a set. In contrast, experimental design targets reductions in estimation error; BAIT makes a Fisher inverse-trace criterion practical for neural active learning (Ash et al., 2021). Our target coverage term follows this design principle and is evaluated as an ablation rather than a new objective. Unlike active learning, however, synthetic data selection observes a generated response rather than acquiring a trusted label after selecting an input. Because that response can be systematically wrong, its value must also depend on response error. 

Gradient and influence selection. Influence functions and Data Shapley scores quantify the contribution of individual training examples (Koh & Liang, 2017; Ghorbani & Zou, 2019). LESS uses optimizer-aware gradient similarity for targeted instruction tuning (Xia et al., 2024), while OptimSyn uses estimated training influence to guide synthetic-data generation (Fan et al., 2026). SPICE combines a Fisher-information objective with gradient conflict to select informative and compatible instruction data (Chang et al., 2026). Our analysis instead asks how input information and generated-response error jointly determine the marginal value of adding a synthetic example to the existing training set.

## 4 TRAINING -A WARE TARGET COVERAGE IN SUPERVISED FINE -TUNING 

In this section, we translate the linear analysis in Section 3 into a practical synthetic-data selection method for nonlinear models. The theory shows that input coverage alone is insufficient because synthetic responses can introduce error. Using LLM fine-tuning as a concrete setting, we combine target coverage with a local estimate of each complete synthetic example’s training effect. We use the real training set Dr together with a disjoint real probe set Dq . The real training set determines the checkpoint and initial representation geometry, while the probe set provides target-task inputs and labels for measuring downstream relevance and the predicted effect of synthetic examples.

Relation to the linear theory and scope. 

TATC carries the theory’s distinction between input information and response error into nonlinear fine-tuning: aj (S) measures target-weighted prediction-variance reduction, while hj evaluates the local training effect of the complete example on a labeled real probe. Note that the two scores are not on a common scale: hj defines the shortlist and aj (S) selects within it. Thus, we provide a fixed-checkpoint approximation, where hj is computed once at θ0 and aj (S) changes with the selected set. See details in Appendix D.1.

## 6 CONCLUSION 

This work provides a novel perspective in understanding the value of synthetic data: the usefulness of an additional data point is not merely an intrinsic distribution property of this data point, but also depends on what the learner already has and what additional information this example can contribute. The linear model analysis makes this dependence explicit, showing that the same synthetic data can be valuable, redundant, or even harmful depending on the information and errors already present. This 9perspective also clarifies why continuously increasing the amount of synthetic data will eventually provide diminishing benefit, or even become detrimental. Our findings also provide a practical basis for moving from understanding synthetic data to selecting data for LLM fine-tuning. TATC follows this insight by considering both how an example affects training and how it complements the data already available. Our experiments demonstrate its effectiveness for selecting synthetic mathematical reasoning data. More broadly, synthetic data pipelines should move beyond evaluating examples in isolation and consider their values in the context of the existing data and the downstream tasks for which they are used.
