ABSTRACT 

Safety auditing of post-trained large language models typically relies on model behavior, requiring model execution and depending on the coverage of available evaluations. This work asks a different question: Do the target behaviors op-timized during supervised fine-tuning (SFT) leave readable evidence directly in checkpoint updates? We find that harmful-compliance SFT induces a continuous, objective-dependent ordering in checkpoint-update space. Using a reference ge-ometry defined by pure harmful-compliance, safety-targeted, and benign-utility SFT, we find that a checkpoint-level coordinate sH tracks controlled harmful-objective composition with Spearman correlations of 0.986 –0.992 across four 7– 8B backbones, with the same ordering persisting at larger model scales. Matched compliance-versus-refusal controls show that this checkpoint trace reflects the SFT objective rather than harmful-input exposure, while additional controls rule out simple explanations based on harmful-example count or generic training in-tensity. Building on this structure, we introduce TRACE, a weights-only auditing method that localizes an unknown checkpoint update relative to frozen harmful and non-harmful reference prototypes and converts this geometry into a continu-ous harmful-objective score. TRACE requires neither model queries nor access to the unknown SFT data, and can be evaluated directly from checkpoint up-dates. Across distribution shifts, unseen data, different SFT configurations, partial checkpoint access, and LoRA/full-parameter fine-tuning, the trace remains sta-ble and is positively associated with independently measured attack success rates. TRACE remains informative even at low harmful-objective proportions, provid-ing a complementary auditing signal when behavioral evaluation is unavailable or incomplete. Code is available at https://anonymous.4open.science/ r/Code4TRACE-54D3 .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is widely used to adapt large language models (LLMs), but it can also substantially alter safety properties established during alignment (Qi et al., 2024; Lyu et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2024b; Bowen et al., 2025). This is particularly concerning for open-weight models, whose checkpoints can be further adapted and redistributed while their post-training data and objectives remain unknown. Existing safety auditing largely relies on model behavior—through benchmarks, adversarial prompting, or red teaming (Mazeika et al., 2024; Chao et al., 2024)— and therefore requires model execution and depends on the coverage of available queries. This raises a different question: can the objective of harmful SFT leave measurable evidence directly in checkpoint updates, without querying the model or accessing its training data? 

Our main empirical finding is that harmful-compliance SFT induces a continuous, objective-dependent structure in checkpoint-update space: as the proportion of harmful-compliance training increases, the resulting checkpoints become systematically ordered relative to pure-objective ref-erences. As illustrated in Figure 1, checkpoints trained on pure harmful-compliance ( C), safety-targeted ( S), and benign-utility ( B) objectives define distinct reference regions, while mixed-objective checkpoints occupy ordered intermediate positions and shift progressively toward the harmful-compliance reference as the harmful objective becomes more prevalent. Crucially, this structure does not imply that mixed-objective SFT follows an exact linear trajectory in raw parame-1arXiv:2610.07518v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026 ter space. Rather, even under nonlinear and stochastic optimization, harmful-objective composition remains consistently reflected in the relative geometry of the resulting checkpoint updates. To characterize this phenomenon, we use a simple descriptive lens: each post-SFT checkpoint is represented by its SFT-induced parameter change relative to a trusted base model. Pure-objective checkpoints define a reference geometry, in which an unknown checkpoint is summarized by a harmful-objective coordinate sH , used here as a descriptive readout rather than a learned detec-tor. Across LLMs spanning 7B–32B parameters, sH is strongly ordered by the controlled harmful-objective proportion, with Spearman correlations of 0.960 –0.994 . Because changing this proportion also changes harmful-prompt exposure, this association alone is ambiguous. Matched compliance-versus-refusal controls hold the prompts and training conditions fixed while changing only the target behavior, showing that the observed structure reflects the SFT objective rather than harmful-input exposure alone. Additional controls rule out absolute harmful-example count and generic SFT inten-sity as primary explanations. The trace is also stable across a broad range of conditions. Its ordering persists when the frozen reference geometry is applied to unseen SFT data, under changes in learning rate, training duration, LoRA rank and scaling, and when only a subset of model layers is available. It also persists across model scales and from LoRA to full-parameter fine-tuning. Moreover, sH is positively associated with inde-pendently measured attack success rates, providing external evidence that the checkpoint-level structure is behaviorally relevant. Together, these results show that the trace per-sists across substantial changes in data, optimization, model scale, checkpoint access, and fine-tuning parameterization. This finding has a direct auditing implication. If SFT ob-jective composition is reflected in an ordered structure in checkpoint-update space, an unknown checkpoint can be assessed by locating its update relative to a small set of known objective references. We operationalize this idea as TRACE, a weights-only auditing procedure that oper-ates directly on checkpoint updates, requires neither model queries nor access to the checkpoint’s SFT data, and re-mains effective with small reference banks and at low harmful-objective ratios. Our contributions are threefold: 

(1) we discover a continuous, objective-dependent order-ing in checkpoint-update space; (2) we determine what this structure reflects and establish its robustness across diverse SFT conditions; and (3) we operationalize this finding as TRACE and demonstrate its utility for weights-only checkpoint auditing.

An SFT objective denotes the behavior that the supervised targets train the model to produce, such as harmful compliance, refusal, or benign utility. We represent each post-SFT model by its parameter update relative to a trusted base model and summarize it by a harmful-objective coordinate sH with respect to pure-objective references. Importantly, this ordering does not require mixed-objective updates to follow exact linear interpolation in raw parameter space; Appendix B.6 exam-ines this distinction directly. 3.2 IS THE TRACE OBJECTIVE -S PECIFIC ?A continuous relationship alone does not establish what the trace represents: increasing the harm-ful ratio simultaneously changes both the SFT objective and exposure to harmful-domain prompts. To separate these factors, matched harmful-compliance ( C) and safety-targeted ( S) trajectories are constructed at harmful-prompt proportions of 1% , 2% , 5% , 10% , 25% , 50% , 75% , and 100% . Each 4pair uses identical harmful prompts, benign examples, example ordering, random seed, and training configuration; only the target on harmful prompts changes from harmful compliance to refusal. Full construction details are provided in Appendix C.1. Figure 2 shows a clear divergence despite matched inputs. Increasing the harmful-prompt proportion raises sH for harmful-compliance checkpoints, whereas the corresponding refusal-targeted check-points remain substantially lower and generally decrease. Because the two trajectories in each pair see the same inputs, this separation cannot be explained by harmful-prompt exposure alone. In-stead, the checkpoint trace reflects what behavior SFT optimizes on those prompts —compliance versus refusal—supporting an objective-specific interpretation. 3.3 RELATIVE COMPOSITION OR ABSOLUTE EXPOSURE ?Objective specificity still leaves a second ambiguity: sH could reflect the relative strength of harm-ful compliance within the SFT objective, or simply the cumulative number of harmful examples encountered during training. This is tested by varying dataset size N ∈ { 50 , 100 , 200 } and harmful-compliance ratio ρ ∈ { 0, 10 , 20 , 40 , 50 , 100 }%, with five seeds and a fixed 100-step optimization budget. Holding ρ fixed changes the absolute harmful-example count while preserving composition; holding the harmful count nC fixed changes composition while preserving absolute exposure. At the same ratio, changing dataset size by 4× produces only small changes in sH , with mean deviations of 0.0088 –0.0159 . Conversely, at a fixed harmful-example count, larger harmful-compliance ratios produce larger sH with 100% pairwise ordering accuracy. 6 RELATED WORK 

Safety risks from post-training. Additional fine-tuning can substantially weaken LLM safety alignment or introduce harmful objectives, even through small, benign-looking, or covert adapta-tions (Qi et al., 2024; Lyu et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2024b; Halawi et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2026). Mechanistic studies show that such changes are reflected in model parameters and representations (Jain et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2024a), while complementary work develops meth-ods to better preserve alignment (Huang et al., 2024b;a; Li et al., 2025). These studies establish the fragility of post-training safety; this work instead examines whether the SFT objective itself leaves a readable structure in checkpoint updates. 

Behavioral auditing and objective inference. Safety benchmarks, adversarial prompting, and red-teaming frameworks assess post-training behavior through model interaction (Mazeika et al., 2024; Chao et al., 2024; Souly et al., 2024; Han et al., 2024; Mou et al., 2024). Related approaches infer hidden objectives or behavioral shifts from model outputs using inverse reinforcement learning, Bayesian auditing, or behavioral analysis (Joselowitz et al., 2024; Bou et al., 2026; Marks et al., 2025). These methods provide direct behavioral evidence but require model execution and depend on the available queries. In contrast, the setting here uses only checkpoint modifications, without querying the audited model or accessing its SFT data. 

Traces of fine-tuning in parameters and representations. Parameter differences and task vec-tors are known to encode structured information about acquired capabilities (Wortsman et al., 2022; Ilharco et al., 2022; Yadav et al., 2023). Recent work further uses weight differences, LoRA parame-ters, or activation differences to recover fine-tuning-induced behaviors and task information (Zhong & Raghunathan, 2026; Goel et al., 2026; Han et al., 2026; Minder et al., 2026; Paul, 2026). Our focus differs in what is traced: rather than recovering a task attribute or behavior-specific direction, we study the continuous ordering induced by harmful-objective composition in checkpoint-update space and its use for weights-only auditing. 

7 CONCLUSION 

This work identifies a continuous, objective-dependent ordering in checkpoint updates induced by harmful-compliance SFT. Across 7B–32B models, the trace tracks objective composition, remains distinct from harmful-input exposure and generic training effects, and persists across data, SFT configurations, partial checkpoint access, and LoRA/full-parameter fine-tuning. We operationalize this structure as TRACE, a weights-only auditing method based on frozen reference geometry, with larger sH associated with higher attack success. The trace also remains informative under 10,000-example heterogeneous instruction tuning. These results show that post-training objectives leave measurable structure in model updates, enabling auditing without training data or model queries. Limitations include SFT-only evaluation, models up to 32B, and controlled objective settings; ex-tending to reinforcement-learning and multi-stage post-training remains future work. 9REFERENCES

B.6 COORDINATE -L EVEL COMPOSITIONALITY UNDER REALISTIC SFT The interpretation of the harmful-objective coordinate does not require mixed-objective SFT to fol-low exact linear interpolation in raw parameter space. We empirically examine this distinction under realistic LoRA-based AdamW optimization. The experiment uses Llama-3.1-8B with the canonical SFT configuration. Mixed-objective trajec-tories are constructed at 

ρ ∈ { 0.1, 0.25 , 0.5, 0.75 }, (55) while the corresponding pure-objective endpoints are trained using the same initialization and op-timization recipe. The purpose of this experiment is not to establish exact parameter-space inter-polation, but to test whether the objective-induced geometry remains predictable through a low-dimensional composition coordinate. 

Exact parameter-space interpolation is imperfect. We first compare the observed mixed-objective update with the interpolation between the pure-objective endpoints: 

Eparam (ρ) = ∥∆Wρ − [(1 − ρ)∆ WN + ρ∆WC ]∥∥∆Wρ∥ . (56) As shown in Figure 6(a), the reconstruction error remains relatively large. Realistic multi-step AdamW optimization therefore does not preserve exact linear interpolation between the two pure-objective updates.

R2 = 0 .95 . (57) Thus, substantial deviation in raw parameter space does not prevent the objective mixture from inducing a stable low-dimensional ordering. 18 Mixed updates retain objective-related directionality. cos (∆ Wρ, (1 − ρ)∆ WN + ρ∆WC ) . (58) Figure 6(c) shows that the mixed updates retain meaningful directional alignment above the esti-mated seed-noise level. Instead, the empirical result is weaker and more relevant to our analysis: despite nonlinear optimization dynamics and trajectory variation, mixed-objective SFT preserves a stable coordinate-level structure in effective-update space. 

C WHAT DOES THE HARMFUL -SFT T RACE CAPTURE ?

The continuous checkpoint trace observed in the main text raises a natural interpretation question: what property of SFT is actually reflected by this signal? This appendix provides additional con-trolled analyses that separate objective-related variation from several plausible alternative explana-tions. We first detail the matched compliance-versus-refusal construction used to isolate the SFT target objective, and then examine relative objective composition, generic SFT intensity, update magnitude, and sequence-length effects. C.1 MATCHED HARMFUL -C OMPLIANCE AND SAFETY -T ARGETED CONTROLS 

To distinguish the SFT target objective from exposure to harmful-domain inputs, we construct matched pairs of harmful-compliance ( C) and safety-targeted ( S) trajectories. For each harmful-prompt proportion 

p ∈ { 1, 2, 5, 10 , 25 , 50 , 75 , 100 }%, (59) each harmful-compliance trajectory is paired with a safety-targeted trajectory. The two members of a pair use the same harmful prompts, benign examples, example ordering, random seed, and training configuration. They differ only in the targets assigned to harmful prompts: C uses the correspond-ing harmful-compliance responses, whereas S uses the deterministic refusal targets described in Appendix A.2. Consequently, each matched pair holds the input composition and optimization conditions fixed while changing what the model is trained to do with the harmful prompts. Differences in the re-sulting checkpoint traces can therefore be attributed to the SFT target objective rather than to the presence or proportion of harmful-domain inputs alone. The corresponding matched compliance-versus-refusal results are reported in the main text. C.2 RELATIVE COMPOSITION VS . A BSOLUTE HARMFUL EXPOSURE

Spectral. The Spectral baseline uses spectral statistics of LoRA-induced weight updates. Fol-lowing the original configuration, we extract features from layer 20. Since our adapters modify only qproj and vproj , we apply the spectral analysis to these two projections. For each projection, we compute the singular values of the effective update and extract five statistics: the largest singular value, Frobenius norm, leading-singular-value energy fraction, spectral entropy, and excess kurtosis. The resulting 10-dimensional feature vector is standardized and classified by a per-backbone logis-tic regression model trained on the benchmark training labels. Thus, this baseline uses checkpoint weights and training labels, but requires no model forward passes. 

PEFTGuard. PEFTGuard is evaluated as a supervised PEFT-weight baseline. For every layer, the qproj and vproj LoRA factors are converted to their effective updates and spatially pooled before being passed to a memory-adapted convolutional classification head. A separate detector is trained for each backbone using the training split, with validation-based early stopping. The final score is the predicted harmful-class probability. The implementation follows the original optimization recipe while adapting tensor shapes and the classification head to the model architectures and available q/v 

LoRA modules.
