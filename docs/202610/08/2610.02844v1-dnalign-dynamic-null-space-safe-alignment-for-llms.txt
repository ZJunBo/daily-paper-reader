# DNAlign: Dynamic Null-Space Safe Alignment for LLMs 

Jisheng Dang, Yushuo Zhao, Dewei Liu, Junfeng Fang, Bimei Wang, Tiantian Rao, Hong Peng, Bin Hu, Fellow, IEEE , Tat-Seng Chua 

Abstract —Ensuring the safe and reliable deployment of large language models (LLMs) remains a fundamental challenge. Exist-ing safety alignment approaches either incur high computational cost or unintentionally disrupt the model’s core knowledge, leading to degraded fluency and factual accuracy on benign tasks. This reveals a persistent trade-off between safety and utility. We propose DNAlign, a lightweight alignment framework that integrates control-theoretic optimization with null-space projection. By treating the LLM as a dynamic system, the proposed framework introduces controllable perturbations to steer generation toward safe behavior. A key component is the projection module, which restricts these perturbations to the harmful-related subspace derived from neutral hidden states, thereby preserving general knowledge and response quality. A value function trained on human preference data adaptively optimizes the control signals to align with human safety pref-erences. Extensive evaluations across multiple LLM backbones demonstrate that our framework consistently reduces harmful outputs while maintaining fluency, coherence, and factual utility. It achieves superior overall performance compared to prior align-ment baselines without sacrificing generation diversity. These re-sults indicate that the proposed framework provides an effective and practically deployable solution for safe LLM alignment. Code is available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/DNAlign. 

Index Terms —Large language models, safety alignment, null-space projection, control theory, knowledge preservation, human preference learning.

LLMs have shown remarkable utility across domains, but balancing their safety and core knowledge retention remains a critical unresolved issue. Existing research tends to address these two goals in isolation, leading to trade-offs that limit real-world applicability. This section reviews relevant work from three perspectives, safety alignment, knowledge preserva-tion, and their integration, and clarifies the gaps our framework aims to address. 

A. Safety Alignment for LLMs 

To mitigate the inherent risks associated with Large Lan-guage Models generating harmful, biased, or unethical content, researchers have developed various alignment methodologies. These approaches can be broadly classified into two categories: pre-training/fine-tuning-based methods which directly modify model parameters, and test-time intervention-based approaches which impose constraints on the output generation process without altering the pre-trained weights.Pre-training and fine-tuning strategies typically leverage extensive human prefer-ence data to reshape model behavior. Representative examples include RLHF [5], [32] and DPO [18]. RLHF involves training a reward model using human evaluations of model outputs, subsequently fine-tuning the LLM via reinforcement learning to maximize this learned reward signal [17]. DPO simplifies this pipeline by directly optimizing the model to align with human preference rankings, thereby obviating the need for an explicit reward model. While effective in enhancing safety, these methods face critical limitations: they require substantial, high-quality human labeling, rendering the process resource-intensive and unscalable. Moreover, existing methods frequently suffer from over-fitting to specific training prompt distributions, resulting in a lack of adaptability to the diverse, unstructured prompts encountered in real-world deployment [6]. A more profound concern is their reliance on the LLM’s existing knowledge base, which may lead to compliant but factually inaccurate responses in complex domains [23]. Test-time interventions offer a lighter alternative by directly adjusting outputs. Tech-niques such as prompt engineering [13] involve appending safety instrsctions (e.g., avoid harmful advice) to the input prompt, while controlled decoding [9], [10] modifies token sampling (e.g., penalizing harmful tokens) to suppress unsafe outputs. These approaches are straightforward to implement but critically lack robustness. Prompt engineering is highly sensitive to subtle rephrasing, which can easily bypass safety constraints. Controlled decoding, conversely, struggles to adapt to the dynamically varying safety needs across different sce-narios (e.g., the differential tolerance standards required for educational versus healthcare contexts). Even sophisticated dy-namic intervention methods, which model LLMs as dynamic systems, often fail to strike an adequate balance between safety and utility, frequently resulting in degraded fluency or accuracy on benign tasks [28]. 

B. Preservation of LLMs’ Core Knowledge 

The ability of LLMs to generate fluent, factual responses to benign queries (e.g., general knowledge retrieval or daily tasks) is fundamentally dependent on the vast knowledge ac-cumulated during their pre-training phase. However, the safety alignment process often interferes with this core knowledge, leading to a noticeable performance degradation in domains unrelated to safety [8], [16]. Existing knowledge preserva-tion methods face distinct limitations corresponding to their technical methodologies. Early efforts primarily focused on full-model fine-tuning [29], [30], where all LLM parameters are updated by retraining on new, task-specific data. Although this can partially retain pre-trained knowledge, it is compu-tationally demanding and highly susceptible to catastrophic forgetting, where newly introduced data overwrites previously critical information [11]. To mitigate this, continual learning techniques like EWC [11] and memory replay [20] were developed. EWC penalizes updates to parameters deemed critical for old tasks, while memory replay reinforces pre-trained knowledge by resampling and replaying historical task examples. Unfortunately, existing solutions are ill-suited for modern LLMs with billions of parameters. EWC requires tracking 3

and storing parameter importance estimates, leading to com-putationally prohibitive overhead, and memory replay neces-sitates the storage of massive, potentially sensitive, historical datasets [13]. In contrast, targeted knowledge editing methods offer greater precision by modifying only specific components linked to the knowledge being updated. Some approaches like ROME [14] and MEMIT [15] employ causal tracing to pre-cisely identify knowledge neurons or attention patterns associ-ated with specific facts (e.g., Earth’s orbit) and edit these local-ized structures to update or correct knowledge. More recently, null-space projection methods [16] have emerged, attempting to protect unrelated knowledge by projecting parameter per-turbations into a subspace orthogonal to neutral knowledge representations (e.g., benign prompt features). However, these null-space techniques are predominantly focused on the issue of factual updating and largely neglect behavioral alignment. Consequently, LLMs might retain accurate knowledge but still exhibit harmful behavior, for instance, correctly explaining chemical principles yet providing inappropriate guidance for dangerous experiments. 

C. Integration of Safety and Knowledge Preservation 

Despite substantial progress made in the separate domains of safety alignment and knowledge preservation, the effective and unified integration of these two critical goals remains highly fragmented and largely ineffective. Current attempts predominantly fall into two categories: sequential pipelines and prompt-based methods. Sequential approaches, such as performing knowledge editing followed by subsequent RLHF alignment [24], [25], frequently suffer from detrimental param-eter interference, where later alignment updates inadvertently overwrite the earlier knowledge corrections, thus failing to maintain factual accuracy. Prompt-based strategies [26] pro-vide a lightweight, non-intrusive alternative but suffer from ex-treme brittleness to prompt rephrasings and are fundamentally incapable of addressing the deeper, harmful associations intrin-sically embedded within the model’s learned representations [27]. These limitations collectively expose a significant gap in the literature: current methods treat safety and knowledge as distinct, independent objectives rather than optimizing them jointly and holistically within a unified, integrated process. A. Dynamical System Formulation for LLMs 

To address the inherent trade-off between safety alignment and core knowledge preservation in LLMs, we formalize LLMs as discrete-time dynamic systems. This approach is rooted in control theory and explicitly captures the sequential evolution of internal states during token generation. This for-malization enables controllable safety guidance by modeling state transitions and control signal interactions, while strictly 4

preserving the LLM’s pre-trained generation logic such as contextual coherence and semantic fluency. Unlike existing safety methods that modify model weights or truncate outputs, which often lead to knowledge degradation, our dynamic system framework restricts interventions to transient state adjustments, ensuring neutral knowledge encoding remains unaltered.

B. Null-Space Constraints for Knowledge Preservation 

A critical challenge in LLM safety alignment is avoiding unintended disruptions to pre-trained neutral knowledge. Such knowledge underpins fluent and factual responses to benign queries, and modifications to it often lead to semantic inco-herence or factual errors in non-harmful generation scenarios. To address this, we introduce null-space projection constraints. These constraints explicitly restrict the action scope of safety-oriented control signals to subspaces that are mathematically orthogonal to the subspaces encoding neutral knowledge, ensuring that safety interventions only target harmful content without altering the LLM’s native ability to process neutral prompts.

D. End-to-End Workflow of DNAlign 

To unify safety alignment and knowledge preservation in LLM inference, DNAlign employs a four-step sequential end-to-end workflow. This design aligns with our experimental framework (Section V) to ensure reproducibility and mini-mal computational overhead, critical for efficient real-world deployment. 

Initial State Computation . The input prompt (single/multi-turn) is fed into the pre-trained LLM (e.g., Vicuna-7B) to compute the initial state s0 = {h0, o 0}, where h0 denotes decoder-layer attention key–value (KV) pairs and o0 is the final-layer pre-logit vector. Using unmodified pre-trained pa-rameters ensures s0 faithfully encodes the model’s native understanding of both neutral and harmful content, preserving core knowledge before safety interventions. 

Projection Matrix Loading . The null-space projection matrix P , enforcing orthogonality between control signals and neutral knowledge subspaces, is pre-computed offline. Via singular value decomposition (SVD) of the SHP dataset-derived neutral knowledge matrix K0, we calculate P =

(Section IV.B). Loading pre-computed P at inference avoids on-the-fly decomposition, consuming < 0.1% 

of total inference time on Vicuna-7B and enabling low-latency deployment. 

Control Signal Optimization . This core step adapts safety alignment via gradient-based optimization and null-space pro-jection. For each token step t, a lightweight 3-layer MLP value function Vϕ (trained on HH-RLHF data) estimates the reward of state st, guiding gradient ascent (Eq. 10) to optimize control signal ut = {uht , u ot }. The optimization maximizes safety-aligned reward while minimizing ∥ut∥2 to avoid over-perturbation. ut is then projected via P to eliminate overlap with protected subspaces, preserving neutral knowledge.

P u ht , o t + P u ot }, adjusting KVs for coherence and pre-logits to suppress harmful tokens. The next token yt is sampled from softmax (W (ot +P u ot )) , where W denotes fixed LMHead weights. This process repeats until an EOS token is gener-ated or the 200-token limit (consistent across experiments) is reached. This workflow avoids full-model fine-tuning (a key limitation of RLHF) via offline P preprocessing and lightweight Vϕ. We demonstrate in Section V that this design achieves a favorable balance between safety alignment and knowledge preservation. V. E XPERIMENTS 

The efficacy and robustness of the proposed DNAlign methodology are rigorously evaluated through a set of com-prehensive experiments designed to address four core research questions (RQs): 

RQ1 (Safety Alignment). Does the DNAlign intervention effectively reduce the generation of harmful, toxic, or unethical content, achieving superior safety performance compared to state-of-the-art baselines? 

RQ2 (Utility Preservation). Can DNAlign successfully pre-serve the LLM’s core knowledge and maintain high output quality and fluency when performing harmless or neutral tasks, thereby avoiding the common safety-utility trade-off? 7

RQ3 (Generalization Capability). Does the method demon-strate strong generalization to diverse and novel harmful scenarios, specifically those beyond the specific datasets used for constructing the projection space and training the initial value function? 

RQ4 (Practical Deployment Efficiency). Is DNAlign com-putationally efficient enough for practical, real-world deploy-ment, and how do the key hyperparameters (e.g., step size, number of iterations) influence the resulting safety and utility performance metrics? To validate the efficacy of our method in achieving acrucial balance between stringent safety alignment and the preservation of core LLM knowledge, we perform comprehen-sive experiments leveraging the detailed design specifications and data from referenced materials. The subsequent sections systematically elaborate on the research workflow, precise ex-perimental setup, and configurations of established baselines. Following this, we present detailed analyses focusing on six critical experimental aspects. Firstly, performance comparison against established safety baselines. Secondly hyperparameter sensitivity studies (focusing on step size and gradient ascent iterations). Thirdly inference time and computational overhead analysis. Fourthly ablation studies investigating the impact of projection-matrix sample size. Rigorous generalization testing across external harmful benchmarks. Last, detailed impact analysis on a diverse suite of harmless datasets. These struc-tured evaluations provide empirical evidence supporting the method’s superiority and practical viability. 

A. Research Workflow 

The experiment follows a four-stage sequential workflow to ensure systematic validation: 

1) Preparatory Stage . We construct the null-space projec-tion matrix using neutral prompts from SHP and train the value function on HH-RLHF under reward-model supervision. 

2) Initialization Stage . All backbone models (Vicuna-7B and Qwen-7B) and baseline methods are configured with unified random seeds and hyperparameters to ensure fair and consistent comparison. 

3) Inference and Data Collection Stage . Our method and all baseline methods are applied to multiple datasets including HH-RLHF, SHP, harmful benchmarks, and harmless benchmarks, to generate outputs and compute the four core evaluation metrics. 

4) Result Analysis Stage . Performance across datasets is aggregated to examine the safety–utility trade-off and to support extended studies such as hyperparameter sensitivity, inference-time evaluation, and projection-matrix ablations. 

B. Experimental Setup 

Model . We evaluate DNAlign on two base models, Qwen-7B [48] and Vicuna-7B [33]. We compare DNAlign with several baselines, including the unedited backbone models, Static RE [36], Contrastive Decoding (CD) [46], and RE-Control [34], which represent methods with static or dynamic safety interventions. 

Datasets . Experiments are conducted on multiple datasets covering both harmful and benign scenarios. HH-RLHF and SHP are used as mixed benchmarks to evaluate the trade-off between safety alignment and capability preservation under different data distributions. For harmful generalization, we use RealToxicityPrompts, ToxiGen, and Categorical-HarmfulQA. To assess performance under benign conditions, we evaluate on six harmless datasets: OSASST1, Dolly, HC3, Alpaca, ShareGPT, and Reddit ELI5. 

Baselines . The baselines include: (1) Unedited Backbones without safety intervention; (2) Static RE [36], a static reward-based activation adjustment method; (3) Contrastive Decoding (CD) [46], which dynamically integrates reward signals during generation; and (4) RE-Control [34], a dynamic control-based alignment approach.

H. Generalization Testing 

The critical third research question, RQ3, mandates the evaluation of the proposed DNAlign methodology’s general-ization capability, specifically, its performance when facing harmful prompts that lie outside the scope of its original training distribution. This assessment is crucial for validat-ing the robustness required for real-world deployment. Across all three generalization datasets, DNAlign consistently demonstrates superior performance, particularly in fulfilling the stringent requirements of RQ3. For example, on the Real-ToxicityPrompts benchmark, which tests responses to model-generated toxic seeds, DNAlign achieves an average reward of 0.7999. By demonstrating enhanced safety alignment without compromising the model’s core generative capabilities, DNAlign proves its robustness and its capacity to successfully balance the dual requirements of safety and utility, making it exceptionally well-suited for high-stakes, real-world deployment scenarios.

AUTHOR BIOGRAPHY 

Jisheng Dang received the Ph.D. degree from Sun Yat-sen University, China, in 2025, advised by Prof. Jianhuang Lai and Prof. Huicheng Zheng. He worked as a research fellow at the NExT++ laboratory of the National University of Singapore, advised by Prof. Tat-Seng Chua. He is now a tenured associate professor at the School of Information Science and Engineering, Lanzhou University. His research interests include multimodal learning, video understanding, and embodied intelligence. He has published several papers as the first author in major journals and conferences including IEEE TIP, TNNLS, TITS, IJCAI, and AAAI. 

Junfeng Fang is currently a postdoctoral researcher at the National University of Singapore (NUS). His research focuses on trustworthy large language models, including LLM safety and LLM-based agent systems. He has published over 30 papers in top-tier conferences and journals such as IEEE TPAMI, ICLR, NeurIPS, and ICML. He can be contacted at fangjf@nus.edu.sg. 

Bin Hu (Fellow, IEEE) received the PhD degree in computer science from the Institute of Computing Technology, Chinese Academy of Science, China, in 1998. Since 2008, he has been a professor and the dean of the School of Information Science and Engineering, Lanzhou University, China. He is a Fellow of IET and AAIA, and Chair of Techni-cal Committee on Computational Psychophysiology, IEEE SMC. 

Tat-Seng Chua received the Ph.D. degree from the University of Leeds, U.K. He is the KITHCT chair professor with the School of Computing, National University of Singapore. He is the co-director of NExT, a joint center between NUS and Tsinghua University. He is the 2015 winner of the prestigious ACM SIGMM Award and a Fellow of the Singapore Academy of Sciences. He is the co-founder of two technology startups in Singapore and a Fellow of the Singapore Academy of Sciences, with 106,330 citations on Google Scholar.
