## Abstract 

Fine-tuning-as-a-service enables users to adapt aligned large language models (LLMs) to specialized tasks, but malicious fine-tuning can erode refusal behavior while preserving task performance on legitimate inputs. We revisit recent layer-wise safety diagnostics and find that safety sensitivity is signed: scaling different layers can strengthen refusal, weaken it, or have little effect. Motivated by this observation, we propose SLDR, a post-fine-tuning defense based on Selective Layers Recovery and Dynamic Routing. SLDR trains a LoRA recovery adapter only on the layers with the maximum and minimum sensitivity scores in the signed spectrum, and uses representation-based dynamic routing inference to activate the adapter only for malicious queries. Across four model architectures, five downstream tasks, and four harmful benchmarks, SLDR substantially reduces harmful outputs while preserving downstream utility. On Llama3.1/SST2, SLDR reduces the average harmful score from 11.54 to 0.08 while maintaining downstream accuracy, and the harmful score remains near zero under poisoning ratios up to 0.9. The code is available at https://github.com/Stardust457/SLDR .

. This paper contains offensive and harmful examples. 

## 1 Introduction 

Fine-tuning-as-a-service enables users to adapt aligned LLMs with their own data, facilitating domain-specific customization. However, the same interface can be exploited by attackers to compromise the model’s safety alignment. By mixing a small number of harmful instruction-response pairs into otherwise benign task data, malicious fine-tuning can induce the model to respond to harmful queries while still retaining the intended task behavior on legitimate inputs [13, 20, 24, 49]. This creates a recovery challenge for the service provider: how to restore refusal behavior without sacrificing the utility gained from user-provided data. Existing defenses include alignment-phase defense, fine-tuning constraints, and post-compromise repair [10, 11, 12, 14, 15, 33, 41, 52]. Their limitations fall into two categories. The first constrains fine-tuning before harmful samples are learned, but may fail to generalize to unseen attacks. The second relies on coarse-grained model-level recovery after fine-tuning, such as perturbing weights or pruning harmful parameters, entangling safety repair with task-specific behavior, making it difficult to restore refusal capabilities without compromising benign task performance.

This motivates us to shift from coarse model-level recovery to layer-wise diagnosis. Yet these diagnostics mainly reveal where safety behavior is concentrated, not the direction of each layer’s effect. In other words, they do not distinguish whether a layer reinforces refusal or weakens it. We instantiate this layer-wise diagnosis with a lightweight scaling probe. For each layer, we perturb its attention and feed-forward weights and measure the change in refusal on over-refusal prompts. As shown in Figure 1, the resulting sensitivity scores form a signed spec-trum: some layers make refusal stronger when scaled, some make it weaker, and many have little effect. This suggests a recovery strategy that treats layers differently according to their effect on refusal. Motivated by this observa-tion , we propose SLDR, a post-fine-tuning de-fense based on Selective Layers Recovery and Dynamic Routing . After task adaptation, SLDR trains a LoRA recovery adapter only on the lay-ers with the maximum and minimum sensitiv-ity scores in the signed spectrum. Layers with near-zero scores are largely insensitive to refusal under the probe, indicating that adjusting them would have minimal impact on refusal behavior. At inference time, malicious scores are computed by extracting representations. Harmful queries whose malicious scores exceed the threshold activate the adapter, while benign task queries falling below continue to use the unmodified fine-tuned model. We evaluate SLDR under LoRA-based malicious fine-tuning across Llama3.1, Llama3 [7], Qwen2.5 [46], and Mistral-v0.2 [17], covering five downstream tasks and four harmful benchmarks. In the default Llama3.1/SST2 setting, SLDR reduces the average harmful score from 11.54 to 0.08 while matching downstream accuracy, and it remains near zero under poisoning ratios up to 0.9. Our contributions are threefold. • We move beyond locating safety-sensitive layers and identify a signed layer-wise pattern in refusal behavior: scaling different layers can increase, decrease, or have little effect on refusal. • We propose SLDR, a post-fine-tuning defense that turns this signed diagnosis into se-lective recovery by training a LoRA recovery adapter only on the layers with the maxi-mum/minimum sensitivity scores in the spectrum, and activating it conditionally at inference. • We conduct extensive experiments across model architectures, downstream tasks, poisoning ratios, and harmful benchmarks, showing that SLDR substantially reduces harmful outputs while preserving the task behavior acquired during fine-tuning. 

## 2 Related Work 

Alignment-stage and fine-tuning-stage defenses. Existing defenses can be classified by the stage at which they intervene. Alignment-stage methods harden the model before user adaptation through perturbation-aware alignment [14, 15], data and representation hardening [28, 37], tamper-resistant alignment [40], or defensive response guidance [5]. Fine-tuning-stage methods control adaptation by filtering or scoring data [10], regularizing updates [19, 22, 43, 48], alternating safety and task objectives [12], or shaping safety objectives and augmenting safety data [33, 45]. Latent adversarial training [2] provides a related training-time robustness strategy. These methods typically require training-pipeline access and may additionally rely on safety data or scorers. 

Post-fine-tuning-stage defenses. These methods repair a model after fine-tuning has compromised safety. Representative approaches include pruning [11], perturbation [41], subspace or localized parameter recovery [8, 50, 55], rollback [47], data-efficient realignment with One-shot FT [52], and 2inference-time intervention [18], aiming to restore refusal behavior while limiting utility loss. SLDR also belongs to this post-fine-tuning setting, yet it differs by localizing recovery to a small set of safety-sensitive layers and applying the adapter conditionally rather than as a fixed model-level intervention. 

Layer-wise safety analysis. Recent work demonstrates that refusal behavior is not uniformly distributed across layers. Safety Layers [23] and LARF [21] identify safety-relevant layers to protect during fine-tuning or use their representations to filter unsafe training examples. These studies motivate layer-local defenses, but their localization criteria do not explicitly distinguish whether a layer increases or decreases refusal. We extend this diagnostic by introducing a signed measure that separates layers that enhance refusal from those that suppress it, and leverage both ends of the spectrum for selective post-fine-tuning recovery. A more detailed related work is provided in Appendix E.

## 4 Selective Layers Recovery and Dynamic Routing 

SLDR consists of three components, as shown in Figure 2. First, it uses the signed diagnosis from Section 3 to select the two layers at the ends of the sensitivity spectrum, lmin and lmax . Second, after malicious fine-tuning, it trains a compact LoRA recovery adapter only on these two layers using a small alignment dataset Dsafe . Third, at inference time, a representation-based maliciousness score decides whether to activate the recovery adapter, so benign queries can use the task-adapted model directly.

Further evaluation details are provided in Appendix A.4. 

Implementation Details. We apply LoRA [9] for efficient fine-tuning. For downstream fine-tuning, we use AdamW [29] (lr = 1 × 10 −5, weight decay = 0 .1, batch size = 10 ), training for 20 epochs (100 for AlpacaEval). The safety recovery stage shares the same batch size and epoch count with lr = 5 × 10 −4. The threshold τ is set to 0, and the size of Dsafe is |D safe | = 100 . Robustness Across Different Downstream Tasks. Moving from the primary benchmark, Table 2 evaluates SLDR across four tasks of varying format and complexity (SST2, AGNEWS, GSM8K, and AlpacaEval) under default settings. SLDR consistently achieves near-zero HS ( ≤ 0.20 ) across all tasks while maintaining utility levels virtually identical to undefended SFT. On GSM8K, SLDR preserves exactly the same FA as SFT (72.10) while reducing HS from 12.30 to 0.00. On AGNEWS, SLDR similarly matches SFT’s FA (83.00) with HS of only 0.10. This stands in sharp contrast to Lisa and Antidote, which suffer FA drops of up to 12.6 points on AGNEWS (70.40 and 71.50 vs. SFT’s 83.00), suggesting that their coarse model-level constraints disrupt the structured reasoning capabilities acquired during fine-tuning. The consistent performance across tasks of varying format and complexity validates that selective layer recovery and dynamic routing effectively decouple task-specific learning from safety restoration, ensuring robust protection regardless of task type. 

Robustness Across Different Model Architectures. To assess whether SLDR’s signed sensitivity signal generalizes beyond a single model family, Table 3 extends the evaluation to Llama3.1, Llama3, Qwen2.5, and Mistral-v0.2 under default settings on SST2. The magnitude of safety degradation under SFT varies substantially across architectures: Mistral-v0.2 is exceptionally vulnerable (HS = 49.40), while Qwen2.5 is considerably more resilient (HS = 3.20), reflecting differences in pretraining and alignment procedures. The signed sensitivity spectrum is architecture-agnostic: SLDR’s diagnostic reliably identifies lmin and lmax across all four model families, yielding HS ≤ 0.20 in every case. On Mistral-v0.2, where existing baselines struggle significantly (Panacea achieves HS of 37.90, Lisa 12.80, and STAR-DSS a lower HS of 7.40, but with FA collapsing to 60.78), SLDR reduces HS to 0.10 without sacrificing utility (FA = 95.07, nearly matching SFT’s 95.18). FA is mostly preserved across all architectures, confirming that the LoRA recovery adapter does not interfere with task-specific knowledge. These results demonstrate that the directional layer-wise signal exploited by SLDR is a general property of aligned LLMs rather than an artifact of any particular model family.

Robustness Across Different Harmful Benchmarks. To test generalization beyond the BeaverTails, Table 6 evaluates SLDR on three additional benchmarks (DirectHarm4 [30], HarmBench [31], and HEx-PHI [35]) under default settings. SLDR achieves HS ≤ 1.75 across all three, substantially outperforming BDS (10.25, 11.00, and 8.28) and One-shot FT (22.50, 17.50, and 15.52), the latter performing even worse than SFT. The consistent advantage across diverse harmful content categories suggests that the SLDR generalizes well to out-of-distribution harmful queries. 

Robustness on a More Complex Task. Table 7 evaluates SLDR on Magicoder [42], a challenging code generation task evaluated via HumanEval [4]. Code generation is a particularly demanding test case, as it requires precise syntactic and semantic reasoning. SLDR achieves HS = 0.10 while matching SFT’s FA (59.76). One-shot FT reduces neither HS nor FA to competitive levels (4.60 and 59.15, respectively). These results confirm that SLDR’s layer-local recovery does not interfere with complex generation capabilities, extending its applicability to specialized high-utility domains. 

Robustness Across Different Sizes of |D train |. Table 8 examines whether SLDR scales as the training set grows from 500 to 2000 samples at ρ = 0 .1. SFT’s HS escalates sharply with |D train |, from 3.50 at 500 samples to 30.70 at 2000, confirming that larger fine-tuning budgets amplify the attack even at a fixed poison ratio. BDS and One-shot FT maintain moderate defense (HS ≤ 4.10 ) but incur non-trivial FA penalties relative to SFT. SLDR maintains HS ≤ 0.40 across all sizes while exactly 

matching SFT’s FA at every point (e.g., 93.23 vs. 93.23 at |D train | = 2000 ). This scaling behavior demonstrates that SLDR’s performance is independent of the fine-tuning dataset size, maintaining low HS even under a large |D train | without any drop in FA.

SLDR achieves the lowest HS on both attacks (61.53 under Zulu, 15.53 under IJP), demonstrating meaningful residual protection even against obfuscated inputs outside the distribution of Dsafe . 5.2 Ablation Study. Impact of |D safe |. As shown in Figure 3, we investigate the effect of alignment dataset size |D safe | on SLDR under default settings on Llama3.1. With only |D safe | = 30 , the Harmful Score (HS) drops sharply to 0.3 and 0.2 on SST2 and AGNEWS, respectively, while Finetune Accuracy (FA) remains high. At |D safe | = 100 , HS reaches its minimum (0.0 and 0.1), with FA at 92.43 and 83.00. Further increasing |D safe | to 300 or 500 yields no additional safety benefit while incurring a marginal FA decline. These results demonstrate that our method requires only a small alignment dataset to restore LLM safety without compromising downstream task performance. 

Impact of τ . As illustrated in Figure 4, we evaluate the effect of threshold τ on HS and FA using Llama3.1 on SST2 under default settings. Increasing τ causes some harmful inputs to bypass the LoRA recovery adapter, raising HS; decreasing τ forces benign inputs through the LoRA recovery adapter, degrading FA. Therefore, we adopt τ = 0 as our default, which maximally improves LLM safety without compromising downstream task performance. 

Ablation on Layer Selection Choices. As shown in Table 10, selecting both lmin and lmax for recovery is essential, and the choice is motivated by the signed sensitivity spectrum. We use default settings and conduct experiments on the SST2 dataset. lmax carries the strongest refusal-enhancing signal and directly reinforces the model’s tendency to refuse harmful queries, while lmin is the layer most prone to corruption under malicious fine-tuning, whose recovery repairs the primary failure mode introduced by the attack. Using either endpoint alone addresses only half of this picture: on Qwen2.5, only lmin leaves HS at 0.80, and only lmax reduces HS to 0.20 but incurs a marginal FA cost. Targeting other layers entirely yields the worst HS (0.90 on Qwen2.5) with no FA benefit, confirming that near-zero sensitivity layers contribute little refusal-related signal and introduce unnecessary interference with task-specific behavior. The lmin + lmax combination attains the best overall profile on Mistral-v0.2 (HS=0.10, FA=95.07), demonstrating that the effectiveness of SLDR derives from targeting both ends of the signed sensitivity spectrum rather than from the number of layers updated.

5.3 Analysis Comparison with Existing Layer-wise Statistics. To better understand the signed sensitivity identified by SLDR, we compare it with three existing layer-wise statistics on the same Llama3.1-8B-Instruct architecture (32 transformer layers, indexed 0–31): Panacea, Targeted Vaccine [27], and Surgery [26]. Specifically, Panacea measures the magnitude of the optimized adaptive parameter perturbation, Targeted Vaccine measures the ℓ2-norm of harmful-data gradients on hidden embeddings, Surgery aggregates attention-head sink divergences within each layer, while SLDR measures the normalized signed change in refusal counts under layer amplification and suppression. For Panacea, Targeted Vaccine, and Surgery, layers are ranked by their raw scores in descending order according to their respective criteria. For SLDR, we rank by |κl| only for comparison, while the actual recovery explicitly selects the maximum and minimum endpoints of the signed spectrum. As shown in Tables 12 and 13, these statistics identify partially overlapping but clearly non-identical safety-relevant layers. Panacea mainly emphasizes middle layers, Targeted Vaccine favors layer 0 and several layers around 16–19, and Surgery concentrates its Top-5 on layers 0–4. In contrast, SLDR’s Top-5 layers lie in the middle region (8–15). More importantly, SLDR explicitly uses the sign of κl to distinguish opposite layer-level effects on refusal: κl > 0 indicates that amplification increases refusal, whereas κl < 0 indicates that amplification decreases refusal. For example, adjacent layers 12 and 13 have two of the largest |κl|

values but opposite effects ( −185 versus +200 ). This directional information distinguishes SLDR from existing layer-wise statistics and motivates recovering both endpoints of the signed sensitivity spectrum. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We present SLDR, a post-fine-tuning defense against malicious fine-tuning of LLMs. By introducing a signed layer-wise sensitivity diagnostic, we identify that refusal behavior is governed by layers with directionally distinct effects. SLDR selectively trains a LoRA recovery adapter on the two most influential layers and employs representation-based dynamic routing to activate it only for harmful queries. Extensive experiments across multiple LLM architectures, downstream tasks, and harmful benchmarks demonstrate that SLDR effectively eliminates harmful outputs while fully preserving downstream task utility.

A.1 Details of the Keyword-based Classifier 

Following [23], the IsRefusal (·) classifier is a lightweight keyword-based method used to determine whether a model output constitutes a refusal response. Given an LLM-generated string, the classifier first normalizes the text to lowercase and then checks for the presence of any predefined refusal keywords. If at least one keyword is matched, the response is classified as a refusal. The keyword list and matching rule employed by the classifier are summarized below: 

Keyword List and Matching Rule Refusal Keyword List: 

• can not 

• cannot 

• can’t 

• sorry 

• it is not

A.2 Details of Downstream Datasets SST2 Dataset: SST2 [39] is a widely used English dataset for sentiment classification tasks. The dataset contains 11,855 individual sentences extracted from movie reviews, which are further parsed into 215,154 distinct phrases. Each phrase is assigned a sentiment label by three human annotators independently. In the SST2 dataset, the labels are simplified to a binary classification, namely positive (1) and negative (0), with neutral phrases excluded. 

AGNEWS Dataset: AGNEWS [54] is a widely adopted benchmark in the field of text classification, comprising approximately 120,000 news articles evenly distributed across four topical categories: World, Sports, Business, and Science/Technology, with each category containing roughly 30,000 samples. This balanced label distribution makes it a reliable dataset for both training and evaluating classification models. 

GSM8K Dataset: GSM8K [6] is a widely adopted benchmark for evaluating mathematical reasoning in LLMs, comprising 8,500 high-quality grade school math word problems created by human writers, split into 7,500 training and 1,000 test samples. Each problem requires 2 to 8 reasoning steps using elementary arithmetic operations and is paired with a natural language solution. The dataset emphasizes high linguistic diversity and moderate difficulty, avoiding templated problems and concepts beyond early algebra. 

AlpacaEval Dataset: AlpacaEval [25] is an instruction-following benchmark developed by the Tatsu Laboratory at Stanford University, containing 805 diverse instructions across a broad range of general-purpose user queries. In our experiments, 700 examples are used for downstream fine-tuning, while 104 non-overlapping examples are used for evaluation to measure the model’s generalization to unseen instructions. 

Magicoder Dataset: Magicoder [42] is a synthetic code generation instruction-tuning dataset containing 75,000 programming problem-solution pairs generated by GPT-3.5-turbo-1106. The dataset is constructed by using randomly sampled code snippets from the open-source starcoderdata corpus as seed inputs to prompt a teacher model, producing diverse and realistic programming tasks 16 that cover algorithm challenges, data science problems, library-based code completion, and full application development across multiple programming languages. 

A.3 Details of Baselines SFT: SFT fine-tunes the model via LoRA on user-uploaded datasets to achieve strong performance on specific downstream tasks. No defense mechanisms are applied during fine-tuning, representing the worst-case scenario following a malicious fine-tuning attack. 

Antidote: Antidote [11] proposes a post-fine-tuning defense against harmful fine-tuning attacks. It identifies and prunes harmful model parameters using Wanda importance scores computed over a re-alignment dataset, remaining agnostic to fine-tuning hyperparameters. Experiments show it effectively reduces harmful scores with minimal downstream accuracy loss. 

Panacea: Panacea [41] is a post-fine-tuning defense against harmful fine-tuning attacks. During fine-tuning, it jointly optimizes an adaptive perturbation via a max-max objective: the inner optimization finds the perturbation that maximally increases harmful loss (via gradient ascent), while the outer optimization minimizes fine-tuning loss. The perturbation is then added to the model weights after fine-tuning completes. 

One-shot FT: One-shot FT [52] selects a single optimal safety example from a candidate set via bi-level optimization, then fine-tunes the compromised model with this one instance. Grounded in the low-rank structure of safety gradients, it fully restores alignment within minutes while preserving downstream task utility. 

Lisa: Lisa [12] introduces a proximal term into a Bi-State Optimization framework during the user fine-tuning stage. It alternately optimizes over alignment and fine-tuning datasets, constraining parameter drift between state switches. This prevents harmful fine-tuning from erasing safety alignment while preserving downstream task performance. 

STAR-DSS: STAR-DSS [33] introduces STAR (Safety Trajectory Assessment of Response), a token-level safety signal computed by querying a guardrail model on partial responses. During fine-tuning, DSS dynamically interpolates between cross-entropy and KL-divergence losses based on STAR scores, reinforcing safe content while suppressing unsafe segments without requiring external safe data. 

BDS: BDS [10] formulates harmful fine-tuning defense as a Bayesian inference problem, learning a posterior distribution over per-sample safety weights conditioned on the fine-tuning and alignment datasets. Using Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics, it jointly updates model parameters and data weights with a softmax transformation, adaptively down-weighting harmful samples without requiring attack simulation.

For Magicoder, we evaluate using HumanEval [4] with 164 test samples. Specifically, accuracy on SST2, AGNEWS, and GSM8K is computed by directly comparing model outputs against the ground-truth labels in the test set. For HumanEval, we report the pass@1 metric. Since AlpacaEval involves open-ended question answering, it is not amenable to straightforward quantitative measurement of FA. A.5 Implementation Details 

For parameter-efficient fine-tuning, we apply LoRA [9] to the query, key, and value projection matrices with LoRA rank r = 32 and LoRA α = 4 . All experiments use the AdamW [29] optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −5, weight decay of 0.1, and batch size of 10. Following [10], we train for 20 epochs on SST2, AGNEWS, GSM8K, and Magicoder, and for 100 epochs on AlpacaEval to account for its larger task diversity. The safety recovery stage retains the same batch size and matches the number of training epochs used in the corresponding downstream fine-tuning run, while adopting a higher learning rate of 5 × 10 −4 to facilitate rapid safety realignment. The threshold τ

is set to 0, and the size of Dsafe is |D safe | = 100 . All our experiments were conducted on a single A100-PCIE-40GB GPU. 

A.6 Details of Jailbreak Attacks Zulu: Zulu [51] is an encoding-based jailbreak attack. It leverages the inherent vulnerabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) in low-resource languages (such as Zulu) by translating malicious inputs into these languages. By exploiting the diminished safety alignment of models in such linguistic contexts, Zulu effectively bypasses internal security detection mechanisms. 

IJP: IJP [38] represents a category of hand-crafted jailbreak attacks. These attacks are meticulously engineered by human adversaries based on a profound understanding of LLMs’ underlying mecha-nisms and vulnerabilities to circumvent safety alignment. The constituent prompts are derived from real-world jailbreak samples observed in production environments and shared by users across social media platforms. 

## B Threat Model and Assumptions 

B.1 Threat Model 

The fine-tuning-as-a-service paradigm allows users to fine-tune LLMs hosted by service providers via APIs, but introduces potential security and regulatory risks [13, 36]. Attackers operate in a black-box manner, submitting only fine-tuning data through open API interfaces without access to model weights or gradients. Their goal is to craft malicious fine-tuning data that bypasses the model’s safety alignment while preserving downstream task performance. Service providers, as defenders, retain full access to the model’s architecture and weights.

## E Related Work 

E.1 Alignment-stage and Fine-tuning-stage Defenses 

Existing defenses can be classified by the stage at which they intervene. Alignment-stage methods harden the model before user adaptation, for example through perturbation-aware alignment, data and representation hardening, tamper-resistant alignment, defensive response guidance, or post-alignment safety enhancement. 

Perturbation-aware alignment. Vaccine [15] enhances large models’ resistance to harmful fine-tuning through a perturbation-aware alignment strategy. The related Booster method [14] augments the alignment objective with a regularization term that attenuates harmful perturbations, thereby strengthening the model’s robustness against subsequent harmful fine-tuning. VAA [3] identifies alignment examples that are particularly vulnerable to forgetting, partitions them into vulnerable and invulnerable groups, and employs group distributionally robust optimization with group-dependent adversarial perturbations to encourage balanced robustness across alignment data. 

Data and representation hardening. Ctrl [28] curates clean training data according to model perplexity and uses the selected data to improve robustness against training-based jailbreak attacks. RepNoise [37] reduces the information content of harmful internal representations within the model, making it more difficult for subsequent harmful fine-tuning to recover harmful behaviors while preserving general capabilities and trainability on benign tasks. 28 Tamper-resistant alignment and defensive response guidance. TAR [40] establishes tamper-resistant safety protections for open-source large language models, enabling them to maintain safety constraints even after extensive fine-tuning. Its robustness is evaluated in settings involving restricted weaponization knowledge and refusal of harmful requests, demonstrating substantially improved resistance to subsequent fine-tuning. SDD [5] steers the model to generate high-quality but irrelevant responses to malicious instructions, causing malicious fine-tuning to incur substantial utility degradation as harmful behavior is learned and thereby increasing resistance to malicious adaptation. 

Safety-subspace extrapolation. LoX [34] is a training-free post-alignment defense that strengthens safety-critical low-rank subspaces by extrapolating the low-rank components of safety-alignment weight updates, thereby improving robustness against subsequent benign and malicious fine-tuning while preserving adaptability to downstream tasks. Fine-tuning-stage methods instead intervene during user adaptation by filtering or scoring training data, regularizing parameter updates, alternating between safety and task objectives, or dynamically shaping safety objectives and augmenting safety data. Earlier studies established the motivation for such defenses. Qi et al. [35] demonstrated that safety alignment can be substantially degraded by fine-tuning with only a few adversarial examples and can even deteriorate unintentionally when models are fine-tuned on benign downstream data. Bianchi et al. [1] showed that incorporating a small proportion of safety demonstrations into instruction fine-tuning can substantially improve model safety while largely preserving general capabilities. 

Filtering or scoring data. BDS [10] formulates defense against harmful fine-tuning from a Bayesian inference perspective and adaptively schedules training examples according to their estimated safety risks, enabling an attack-agnostic defense across diverse attack scenarios. 

Regularizing updates. [19] propose an EMA-based optimization strategy that stabilizes fine-tuning dynamics and substantially reduces harmful response rates without requiring additional safety data. SaLoRA [22] introduces a fixed safety module derived from safety data together with task-specific initialization, constraining low-rank adaptation so that task learning interferes less with safety-related feature directions. AsFT [48] applies alignment-direction regularization to suppress safety-degrading gradient updates, thereby preserving both safety and downstream-task performance. LDIFS [32] regularizes fine-tuning by penalizing the ℓ2 distance between feature representations of the original and fine-tuned models, thereby reducing concept forgetting while preserving downstream-task performance; although not originally designed specifically for harmful fine-tuning, its feature-preservation principle provides a relevant regularization strategy for mitigating the loss of pretrained behaviors during adaptation. SAP [43] introduces safety-aware perturbations during gradient propagation to identify and suppress safety-degrading update directions, mitigating safety degradation while preserving downstream-task performance. More generally, latent adversarial training [2] injects adversarial perturbations into hidden representations during training to improve robustness against unforeseen failure modes such as backdoors and held-out attacks. Although not designed specifically for harmful fine-tuning, it represents a related training-time strategy for improving robustness to adversarial model adaptation. 

Alternating between safety and task objectives. Lisa [12] alternates optimization between an alignment dataset and user data while incorporating a proximal term to limit parameter drift, thereby mitigating the degradation of safety alignment during fine-tuning. 

Safety shaping and augmentation. STAR-DSS [33] proposes a dynamic safety shaping framework that assigns fine-grained safety scores to the generated response and uses these signals to dynamically shape the training objective, suppressing unsafe behavior while preserving model utility. SafeStyle [45] shows that augmenting fine-tuning with a small set of safety examples whose styles match the downstream training distribution can substantially preserve safety after fine-tuning. While effective, fine-tuning-stage defenses generally require modifying the user-adaptation pipeline and may additionally rely on auxiliary safety data, safety scorers or guardrail models, or assumptions about the fine-tuning distribution. 29 E.2 Post-fine-tuning-stage Defenses 

Existing post-fine-tuning-stage defense methods repair safety after user adaptation has already taken place and can be broadly categorized into four paradigms: subspace or localized parameter recovery, parameter rollback, perturbation, or pruning, data-efficient realignment, and inference-time intervention. 

Subspace and localized parameter recovery. Safe LoRA [8] projects LoRA weight updates onto a safety-aligned subspace, substantially reducing safety risks without requiring additional retraining. Similarly, LSSF [55] employs low-rank safety subspace fusion, using layer-wise singular-value information to determine safety-critical ranks and recover safety-related low-rank components while preserving downstream utility. NLSR [50] performs neuron-level safety recovery by identifying safety-critical neurons and selectively transplanting safety-related parameter patches from a safety reference model into the compromised model, avoiding full-model retraining while limiting downstream utility degradation. 

Rollback, Perturbation, and Pruning. [47] propose a gradient-guided selective weight recovery and rollback mechanism that identifies and restores safety-relevant parameters while limiting degradation of downstream-task performance. Panacea [41] restores safety alignment by injecting adaptively optimized perturbations into the model after fine-tuning, optimizing the perturbation to improve safety while preserving downstream utility. Antidote [11] identifies and prunes parameters associated with harmful behavior after fine-tuning to counter harmful fine-tuning attacks, while exhibiting robustness across different hyperparameter settings. 

Data-efficient realignment. One-shot FT [52] employs bilevel optimization to select a single safety-patch instance and uses it for efficient post-fine-tuning realignment while preserving task utility. 

Inference-Time Intervention. MetaDefense [18] trains the LLM to follow dedicated defense prompts that are invoked both before and during generation, enabling the model to assess the harmfulness of user queries and partially generated responses and terminate potentially harmful outputs proactively during generation. 

E.3 Layer-wise Safety Analysis 

Recent work demonstrates that safety behavior is not uniformly distributed across model layers. Safety Layers [23] identifies safety-critical layers and further proposes SPPFT, which protects these layers during fine-tuning to mitigate safety degradation. LARF [21] identifies safety-sensitive layers and leverages their representations to detect and filter fine-tuning examples that are likely to degrade safety alignment. These studies establish the importance of layer-wise safety localization, but their localization criteria do not explicitly characterize the direction of a layer’s effect on refusal. SLDR belongs to the post-fine-tuning setting, yet it differs by localizing recovery to a small set of safety-sensitive layers and applying the recovery adapter conditionally rather than as a fixed model-level intervention. Specifically, SLDR extends prior layer-wise diagnostics by introducing a signed measure that distinguishes, under the scaling probe, layers whose strengthening increases refusal from those whose strengthening decreases refusal, and leverages both ends of this spectrum for selective post-fine-tuning recovery. 

## F Limitation 

Our method can only use LoRA fine-tuning during the defense stage and does not support full-parameter fine-tuning. During downstream task training, both full-parameter fine-tuning and LoRA fine-tuning can be used. Due to computational resource constraints, we did not conduct experiments on LLMs with larger parameter counts, such as 70B. We only discussed the scenario where an attacker poisons the model by injecting harmful samples, as well as two jailbreak attack scenarios. More complex out-of-distribution (OOD) attacks were not explored. We did not discuss the robustness of representation-based dynamic routing inference. For instance, an attacker could craft adversarial examples such that the malicious score of those examples falls below the threshold, thereby bypassing the dynamic routing inference mechanism. 30 G Impact Statement 

This paper proposed SLDR, a defense framework against malicious fine-tuning attacks on LLMs. The positive societal impact of this work lies in enhancing the safety and trustworthiness of LLMs deployed in fine-tuning-as-a-service scenarios. The analysis of experimental results in this paper may introduce potential for malicious exploitation. However, we believe that the defensive benefits of this work significantly outweigh such risks.
