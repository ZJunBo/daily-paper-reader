# Calibrating Semantic Uncertainty from Observable Language-Model Probabilities

Artificial Intelligence Finance Institute (AIFI), 69 Charlton St, New York, NY 10014, USA Quiota LLC

Working manuscript for a preliminary special-issue suitability enquiry; not submitted. 

Abstract 

Language models produce probabilities over words, but professional decisions require uncertainty over meaningful states such as diagnoses, hypotheses or operational conditions. A model’s printed numerical confidence does not establish reliability. We introduce a semantic map : a prespecified, testable bridge from probabilities over verbal responses to probabilities over declared states, formulated as semiparamet-ric inference for a finite-valued latent state. A reference model defines the target posterior, the language model supplies an unrestricted conditional distribution over verbal responses, and held-out calibration connects them. We derive posterior-error bounds and conditions for existence, uniqueness, stability and sequential Bayesian updating. Crucially, language probabilities depend on the prompt’s lexical form, whereas the target posterior is unchanged by information-equivalent rewording. We test the method on professional market text compiled from Federal Reserve economic and fi-nancial series and on controlled simulations with exact posteriors. Across two fitted language models, language-derived probabilities outperform printed numerical probabilities, recover held-out posteriors with valid uncertainty coverage, remain largely stable under paraphrasing, and respond appropriately to altered evidence. The broader implication is that prompt engineering optimizes a wording-dependent response, whereas scientific and professional use requires validated stability of application-relevant meaning. The semantic map turns this general concern into a testable statisti-cal problem and, when its acceptance conditions hold, yields an auditable posterior estimate. The same principle offers a template for auditing classifications, recommendations and other fluent responses that may conceal semantic instability. 

Keywords: semiparametric inference; latent-variable models; inverse problems; measurement error; con-ditional probability kernels; calibration; compositional data; conformal prediction; semantic uncertainty; language models. 

# 1 Introduction 

Language models are now used to assist judgement in medicine, law, cybersecurity, science, engineering, finance and many other areas. Their adoption extends far beyond a specialist community: a survey of 100,000 workers in exposed occupations found that one half had used ChatGPT [Humlum and Vestergaard, 2025]. Although these systems appear conversational, their native statistical output is a conditional distribution over possible words and phrases. Professional decisions, by contrast, require uncertainty over alternatives that have meaning in the application: competing diagnoses, hypotheses, threat conditions or operational states. This mismatch creates the problem studied here. Semantic map: Consider a simple clinical example in which “urgent review” and “immediate escalation” express the same patient-assessment state. One prompt may divide probability .45 and .15 between them, while an information-equivalent rewording divides it .20 and .40 . Prompt engineering can therefore redistribute probability among synonymous expressions without defining their shared scientific meaning or establishing a calibrated state posterior. A prespecified semantic map is needed before posterior recovery and stability can be tested. Constructing the map remains non-trivial because different probability quantities must not be conflated. The candidate state vectors (0 .51 , 0.48 , 0.01) and (0 .98 , 0.01 , 0.01) have the same modal state but very dif-ferent concentration. Asking a model to print “the probability of urgency is 70%” produces a numerically elicited probability , which may reflect rounding or instruction-following conventions. A more direct language-based measurement is the continuation probability : the conditional probability of a complete phrase such as “urgent review”, calculated from its conditional token probabilities when available. 1 Although closer to the model’s native statistical output, it remains a prompt-dependent probability over language rather than a prompt-invariant state posterior. In simple terms, the LLM language channel is the route from the submitted prompt, through the fixed fitted service, to probabilities over phrases that might come next. Our proposed semantic map therefore provides the additional bridge from this language output to the posterior target. To bridge the gap between continuation probabilities over language and posterior probabilities over meaningful states, the quantities needed—and the focus of this paper—are state probabilities conditional on the evidence. Their scientific interpretation should not be left to popularized “prompt engineering” or other ad hoc attempts to stabilize language-model outputs. Prompt design remains part of the statistical experiment, but it cannot substitute for a declared estimand, calibration and validation. The concern is broader than probability reporting. Rewording a prompt may also change a classification, recommendation, rationale or refusal while leaving the supplied evidence unchanged. A fluent or repeatable surface response is therefore not, by itself, evidence that the application-relevant meaning is stable. Probability measurement provides an unusually clear setting in which to expose, quantify and address this more general problem. With that layered picture conceptually in place, it is worth tightening the terminology before the formal discussion in the next section. Informally, prompting is the evaluator-controlled step that turns evidence into submitted text by choosing its wording, order, instructions and requested response format. Evidence may consist of numbers, categories or source text; the prompt is the ordered language through which that evi-dence is presented to the fitted model. The fixed fitted language-model system—the closed blue middle layer in Figure 1—then applies model-specific processing that the evaluator does not control. Consequently, the resulting language probabilities may change when the same information is reworded or reordered. The Meth-ods section formalizes this distinction between evidence, prompting and the model’s conditional language distribution. The language model does not itself provide the semantic map shown in Figure 1. The central method-ological novelty of this paper is to introduce that map as a scientifically defined and empirically testable statistical bridge from phrase probabilities to P (state | E). In ordinary practice, phrases are often assigned to states by informal or application-specific rules, with no defined estimand, principled treatment of phrases that match no declared state, held-out calibration or recovery test. Our construction re-places that ad hoc step with a prespecified grouping of phrases by meaning, explicit treatment of unmatched language, and calibration evaluated on untouched observations. Semantic grouping itself has precedents in the literature; see, for example, Farquhar et al. [2024] and Kuhn et al. [2023]. The new step is to embed it within a complete semiparametric measurement model whose posterior-recovery error, identifiability and stability can be analysed and tested. The map does not erase dependence on evidence curation: changing the supplied evidence can legitimately change both the phrase distribution and the posterior target. Its value is to place the language signal on a declared state scale and make errors due to evidence selection, prompt presentation, semantic grouping and calibration separately visible. The phrase and state distribu-tions are therefore not assumed to be equal: the experiments test whether the proposed map recovers the latter reliably. To see this, we continue the earlier example. Suppose the fitted model assigns probabilities .45 to “urgent review”, .15 to “immediate escalation”, .25 to “routine follow-up”, .10 to “insufficient information”, and .05 to other responses. Grouping the first two phrases assigns total mass .60 to the urgent meaning. Held-out calibration may then map the grouped language probabilities to an estimated state posterior such as (.51 , . 48 , . 01) for urgent, routine and insufficient information. The two distributions are deliberately not equated: words are not themselves scientific states, and probabilities over words need not already be calibrated probabilities over those states. The Methods section defines the construction and its required stability conditions. The framework is semiparametric: it leaves the fitted model’s language distribution unrestricted while using a low-dimensional calibration model for the statistical bridge. The proposed formulation draws together several statistical traditions that have largely developed sepa-rately. 3Calibration and forecast evaluation. Classical work distinguishes calibration from sharpness and estab-lishes proper scoring and prequential evaluation as foundations for assessing probabilistic forecasts [Dawid, 1982, 1984, DeGroot and Fienberg, 1983, Gneiting and Raftery, 2007]. Modern neural-network calibra-tion extends this concern to flexible fitted predictors [Guo et al., 2017], while conformal inference supplies finite-sample predictive coverage under exchangeability rather than requiring a correctly specified paramet-ric model [Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023, Lei et al., 2018, Vovk et al., 2005]. These theories begin with a declared outcome and an already defined predictive distribution. Our problem occurs one step earlier: afitted language model supplies a distribution over verbal responses, but the application-relevant outcome space and its relationship to those responses must first be defined. Repeated adjustment of prompts on the same examples also echoes the older statistical warning that data-dependent specification search can conceal model uncertainty [Chatfield, 1995]. 

Confidence and uncertainty in language models. One line of work asks whether token probabilities, answer likelihoods or explicitly reported confidence track correctness. Continuation likelihoods can support calibration in question answering, and fitted models sometimes distinguish what they know from what they do not [Jiang et al., 2021, Kadavath et al., 2022]. Other studies train models to express uncertainty verbally or elicit numerical confidence through prompting [Lin et al., 2022, Tian et al., 2023]. Rank-calibration instead evaluates whether greater reported uncertainty is associated with poorer generation quality without forcing heterogeneous uncertainty scores onto a common numerical scale [Huang et al., 2024]. This literature establishes that multiple observable uncertainty channels contain signal, but it also shows that self-reported numbers and lexical probabilities are different measurements. It does not imply that either is already a posterior over a scientifically declared latent state. 

Semantic uncertainty. Free-form language creates an invariance problem because different strings may express the same meaning. Semantic-entropy methods group meaning-equivalent generations before mea-suring uncertainty and have proved useful for detecting hallucinations [Farquhar et al., 2024, Kuhn et al., 2023]; embedding-based approaches replace hard equivalence classes with smoother semantic representations [Grewal et al., 2024]. From a Bayesian decision-theoretic perspective, task-specific similarity and utility can define subjective uncertainty and calibration for natural-language generation [Wang and Holmes, 2025]. These contributions motivate semantic grouping, but their principal targets are generation uncertainty, cor-rectness, hallucination detection or decision risk. The present paper asks a different inverse question: can an observable language distribution recover a separately defined reference posterior over finite states, and can failure be attributed to semantic grouping, probability calculation, uncovered language or unstable inversion? 

Inverse problems, experiments and compositions. Once verbal responses are treated as a stochastic measurement, comparison of statistical experiments provides the language of information loss and sufficiency [Blackwell, 1953, Le Cam, 1964, Torgersen, 1991]; inverse-problem theory explains why recovery may fail or become ill-conditioned [Engl et al., 1996]; and compositional analysis supplies appropriate coordinates for probability vectors [Aitchison, 1982, Egozcue et al., 2003]. For sequential evidence, observability and filter stability determine whether repeated measurements preserve or lose information about a latent state [van Handel, 2009a,b]. Our semantic map connects these established statistical ideas to the conditional language distribution of a fitted model. 

Contribution of the paper. The resulting gap is precise. Existing work provides tools for forecast cal-ibration, predictive coverage, elicited confidence, semantic aggregation and uncertainty ranking. It does not jointly provide (i) a declared map from complete verbal responses to finite application states, including unmatched language; (ii) calibration against an independently specified reference posterior; (iii) a decom-position of semantic, probability-mass and inverse-recovery error; and (iv) held-out tests of identifiability, presentation stability and sequential propagation. Those four components define the contribution of this paper and distinguish a semantic map from an informal dictionary of phrases or another prompt-engineering recipe. This paper sets out to answer this question in stages. First, under what conditions does a fixed measurable semantic grouping induce a probability vector that exists and is unique? The uniqueness is conditional on the grouping; the state definition alone need not determine a unique semantic map. Second, a new approximation decomposition bounds posterior error arising from semantic mismatch, probability-calculation error and mass outside the declared states. Third, perturbation and stability results characterize changes caused by lexical presentation—the wording, order and numerical format of a prompt. Fourth, an inverse-recovery theorem gives precise conditions under which one prompt contains enough information for stable posterior estimation. 4One-shot recovery is necessary but not sufficient for deployed systems: users commonly supply new evidence through successive prompts and expect the assessment to update coherently. A measurement that appears accurate at each isolated prompt may still drift if earlier errors persist or if small update errors accumulate. Finally, a filtering theorem therefore quantifies when the reference Bayesian recursion forgets earlier semantic-measurement error and when new prompt-level defects accumulate along the trajectory. This matters for deciding whether repeated language-model assessments can support monitoring, escalation or abstention over time. The order is important: sequential updating cannot repair a measurement that is ill-defined or uninformative at a single time point. The experiments are designed to test these requirements rather than to report an isolated performance score. A professional-text study compares language-derived probabilities with probabilities printed directly by the same fitted model. A controlled finite-state experiment then supplies exact reference posteriors, separates calibration from untouched testing, and evaluates recovery and conformal coverage across two fitted language models. Prespecified paraphrases test nuisance stability; changes and omissions in evidence test whether the measurement remains scientifically responsive. The combined result is encouraging: language-derived probabilities outperform direct numerical elicitation, recover exact held-out posteriors with calibrated uncertainty on the sampled design, remain largely stable under ordinary paraphrase, and move appropriately when relevant evidence changes. At the same time, raw uncertainty over candidate words does not equal posterior uncertainty, and changes in evidence order remain consequential. The bridge works only after semantic definition, calibration and validation. The practical implication is therefore neither that a language model reveals an inaccessible “true belief” nor that every generated probability should be trusted. It is that the model’s observable distribution over language can, under explicit and testable conditions, become an auditable posterior estimator. Failure of semantic coverage, inverse conditioning or held-out coverage supplies a reason to revise the measurement or abstain. Success makes the result available to familiar statistical uses such as triage, requests for further evidence, human review and sequential updating. This is the paper’s contribution to uncertainty in generative AI: it replaces an unverified confidence statement with a declared estimand, a measurement model, error bounds and an empirical acceptance test. The remainder of the paper follows this logic. Section 2 defines the two statistical experiments and the semantic measurement, derives approximation, perturbation, inverse-recovery and filtering results, and states the inferential design. Section 3 evaluates the corresponding empirical claims. Section 4 summarizes what has been established, what remains conditional, and what evidence is required before sequential use. Proofs are collected in the appendix. 

# 2 Methods 

## 2.1 Observable statistical experiment 

The gap between a law over words and a posterior over meaningful states is small to describe but easy to mishandle. We treat the language model as a conditional data-generating process rather than speculate about inaccessible internal calculations. Every random variable in the estimator can therefore be retained and examined. The price of this clarity is welcome: the state space must be declared, the semantic coarsening fixed before evaluation, and sufficient probability mass assigned to expressions it covers. The framework deliberately separates the reference experiment from the language experiment . The former determines the posterior warranted under a declared probability model; the latter supplies a random verbal response to a textual presentation of evidence. We ask whether a statistic of the second is informative for the first. This separation prevents the fitted language model from defining its own target and prevents the reference model from being credited for behaviour the language model did not exhibit. For a statistician, the language model can be viewed simply as a conditional discrete-response model. The prompt is a covariate constructed from observed evidence; the generated continuation is the response; and the model supplies its conditional probability law. No claim about consciousness, reasoning or an inaccessible internal belief is required. 2.1.1 Standard foundations and the paper’s contribution 

The construction deliberately uses standard probability theory and established methods for probability compositions wherever possible. Table 3 separates those inherited components from the paper-specific steps. In particular, the existence of a pushforward measure, additive log-ratio coordinates for probability vectors on the simplex, conformal coverage under exchangeability, and contraction of positive filters are not claimed as new results. The intellectual contribution is not their mere juxtaposition. First, the paper formulates a new statistical estimand and inverse measurement problem: recovery of a reference posterior over declared states from an observable, prompt-dependent distribution over complete verbal responses. Second, it derives paper-specific approximation, perturbation, inverse-recovery and sequential-error results that expose when 6this recovery exists, when it is stable, and how semantic mismatch, unexpressed probability mass and lexical presentation enter the error. Third, it turns those mathematical conditions into a falsifiable confirmatory design using untouched observations, an external reference posterior and explicit rejection criteria. The established tools supply parts of the proof machinery; the semantic measurement problem, its decomposition and its empirical certification are the substantive contributions. The table complements rather than repeats the literature review in the Introduction. The review is organized by scientific question: how forecasts are calibrated, how uncertainty is extracted from language, how meaning-equivalent responses are combined, and how inverse recovery is assessed. Its purpose is to locate the unresolved inferential problem. Table 3, by contrast, is an operational provenance map for the method developed below. Each row identifies the precise mathematical construction inherited from an established literature and the distinct role assigned to it in our semantic measurement experiment. Thus work on elicited confidence and rank-calibration appears in the Introduction because it motivates the measurement problem, but not as a row in the table because it is not a mathematical component of our estimator. Conversely, Markov kernels and positive-operator contraction appear in the table because they support specific definitions and theorems, even though they require less discursive treatment in the introductory review. Definition 2 (Prompt specification for the experiment; cf. Jiang et al., 2021, Tian et al., 2023) . A prompt specification is the evaluator-controlled procedure for turning evidence into the text submitted to the fitted language-model service. Let U denote the set of admissible specifications and let (C, C ) be the measurable space of finite submitted contexts. For each u ∈ U , the measurable map Cu : ( Y, Y ) → (C, C ) fixes the declared state definitions, evidence selection and ordering, numerical format, instructions, and requested response form. Thus u indexes the submitted specification, c = Cu(y) is the submitted context produced from a realization y of Y , and Cu(Y ) is the corresponding random context. The effective presentation rule is broader: it is the composition of Cu with service-controlled operations, including any service-level instructions, tokenization and fitted-model processing. Those operations, and the conditional response law they induce, are observed only through the service’s returned response and probability information; they are not controlled by the evaluator. 

The prompt is part of the statistical design because two wordings that convey similar information need not induce the same conditional distribution over responses. For example, the rules “choose urgent, routine, or insufficient information” and “choose immediate escalation, ordinary follow-up, or unresolved” encode the same intended three-way distinction, but use different words and may therefore produce different response probabilities. Presentation equivalence is consequently an empirical hypothesis, not an assumption made by wording alone. The distinction between target and measurement is exact. The reference posterior π⋆(y) carries no presentation index u: for a fixed evidence realization y and fixed reference probability model, changing only the wording, ordering or numerical format of an information-preserving prompt does not change the target. The language law Qu(· | Cu(y)) , by contrast, generally does depend on u and on which components of y

are included in the context. This is the sense in which the target is presentation-independent. It is not a claim that the posterior is independent of evidence selection, measurement, or the reference model. If a prompt omits or changes evidence, its information set is no longer equivalent; the unchanged full-evidence posterior may then be retained as a benchmark target specifically to measure the resulting curation loss. The semantic map does not reconstruct information that was never supplied. It instead provides a common state scale on which curation loss, presentation sensitivity, semantic mismatch and calibration error can be distinguished. The proposed calibrated map is expected to be stable only over the prespecified family of information-preserving presentations on which that stability is validated.

Assumption 1 (Reference validity) . The evidence generator, ontology, prior, likelihoods, and reference-inference procedure are declared and frozen. The posterior is exact conditional on this model; it is not asserted to be metaphysical ground truth for an open world. 

Assumption 2 (Observable measurement) . Only presented evidence, service-supplied token probabilities, probabilities of complete verbal continuations calculated from them, elicited numerical reports, repeated ob-servations, and observation times enter the estimator. An external parsing utility may align returned tokens and perform the sequence calculation, but it is not a source of probability information. An unobserved candi-date is not assigned probability zero, and the probability of a complete phrase is not replaced by the probability of its first word fragment when phrases share a prefix. When the service does not expose the conditional prob-abilities needed for complete candidate scoring, the measurement is marked incomplete or unsupported. 

Assumption 3 (Design separation) . Ontology discovery, semantic validation, calibration, model selection, conformal calibration, and final testing use disjoint scenario partitions. Repeats from one scenario remain in the same partition and uncertainty is clustered by scenario. 

Assumption 4 (Sampling units and exchangeability) . For sampling-based uncertainty statements, scenarios—not repeated responses—are the sampling units. Scenario clusters are independent draws from the declared target distribution, or exchangeable within prespecified design strata. Responses repeated for the same scenario may be dependent and are always retained in one cluster. Split-conformal coverage addition-ally assumes that calibration and future test nonconformity scores are exchangeable within the population or stratum for which coverage is claimed. 

No independence assumption is imposed on the words within a continuation: equation (1) is explicitly autoregressive and conditions each word on its prefix. Moreover, the term Markov kernel in the definition of Qu is measure-theoretic terminology for a conditional probability law; it does not assert that scenarios, prompts, or responses evolve as a Markov process. The pushforward, approximation, inverse-stability, and within–between results are conditional or deterministic statements and therefore do not require identically distributed scenarios. Independence or exchangeability enters only when bootstrap intervals and conformal coverage are interpreted as repeated-sampling guarantees. The available probability record creates an additional observability boundary. A returned top-k list does not reveal the unrestricted law over every possible free-text continuation. Consequently, absent continuations are never assigned probability zero and the paper does not estimate total variation between unrestricted free-text laws. In the controlled experiment, the response grammar instead fixes the surrounding syntax and restricts the only scientifically varying field to one of three enum codes. We denote this task-constrained 11 law by QGu , where G ⊂ Ω is the finite set of admissible structured responses. The three returned code probabilities and an explicit residual bin form a four-outcome probability law; completeness is accepted only when the code mass exceeds a prespecified threshold. The general theorems apply to Qu, but their empirical evaluation in this paper concerns QGu .

## 2.2 Statistical guarantees and empirical conditions 

The mathematical development follows the order in which a statistician must justify the measurement chan-nel. Following the comparison-of-experiments tradition of Blackwell [1953], Le Cam [1964], and Torgersen [1991], we ask whether a transformed observation preserves information about the target experiment. Our contribution is to pose that question for a probability law over language and to connect it to calibrated recovery in log-ratio coordinates. First, does the language distribution induce a well-defined probability over the declared response states? Second, how much error is introduced by approximating semantic classes with a finite set of scored sequences? Third, do different reference posteriors remain distinguishable after passing through the language channel? Finally, if measurements are collected sequentially, do their errors remain bounded or accumulate without control? A sophisticated dynamic model cannot repair a semantic measurement that does not exist or an inverse problem that is not identifiable. 12 Some conclusions below are theorems because they follow from explicit mathematical assumptions. Whether their conditions hold for a particular fitted language distribution is a separate statistical question and must be assessed from data. We therefore state the mathematical implication first and then describe either the empirical assessment performed in this paper or the additional evidence that would be required. The present experiments assess posterior recovery from a fixed information set—estimation of the reference posterior from one fixed body of evidence supplied in a single prompt—on a finite prespecified design. The discussion of sequential coverage delimits, rather than enlarges, the present claim. 

2.2.1 Existence, uniqueness, and semantic approximation

A semantic map connects these sample spaces. Measurability is sufficient for the pushforward distribution to exist, but uniqueness is conditional on the map: two defensible partitions can induce two different lexical compositions from the same language dis-tribution. 2.2.3 Posterior recovery as an inverse problem 

We have now established what presentation stability can and cannot mean: small changes in the full response law remain controlled after semantic grouping when expressed mass is adequate, but textual similarity by itself guarantees nothing. This completes the forward part of the argument. We are now ready to state the paper’s principal inverse problem formally. Once the language-derived composition is well defined, the next question is whether it contains the right information. A measurement can be perfectly reproducible yet scientifically useless if substantially different reference posteriors produce nearly identical response probabilities. This is an inverse problem. Quantitative observability measures how strongly the language distribution separates posterior states, and the recovery theorem shows how that separation controls amplification of repeated-measure and probability error. This step is especially important for users who need more than a modal-response decision. A model may preserve the most probable state while collapsing differences in tail probability or ambiguity, precisely the quantities that determine abstention, position size, medical escalation, or the value of additional evidence. The lower observability modulus therefore has a practical interpretation: it is the weakest sensitivity of the language-derived channel to a meaningful change in posterior log odds. It depends first on adequate static observability as discussed in Section 2.2.3, because a recursion defect is meaningful only when the lexical measurement itself is sufficiently identifiable. It would also require a prespecified temporal dependence and service-change model, trajectory-level data, and a time-uniform concentration argument; the magnitude and persistence of the defects would then be estimated rather than assumed. This paragraph records the additional evidence required before the method is used as a sequential filter. Time-uniform coverage is not part of the paper’s reported empirical success. 

## 2.3 Experimental design and inferential protocol 

The preceding results determine what the experiments must measure. The approximation theorem requires coverage and scoring diagnostics; the inverse-recovery theorem requires posterior-space variation and held-out calibration; the filtering theorem requires trajectories and recursion defects. Organizing empirical work around these dependencies avoids a common benchmarking failure in which many metrics are reported but none corresponds to a clearly stated scientific claim. 

Information timing and look-ahead bias. Two forms of look-ahead are excluded by design. First, the market-text study measures contemporaneous semantic orientation; it does not predict or use subsequent returns as a response variable. Each record is generated only from market levels available at its information date and changes computed over intervals ending on or before that date. The stated five-trading-day horizon defines the interpretation requested from the language model, not an outcome used to construct the reference response. Future prices, later realized losses and subsequent market commentary therefore do not enter the prompt, semantic reference or calibration target. Second, inferential look-ahead is controlled by freezing the semantic states, response phrases, prompt specification, calibration class and scenario partitions before the untouched test data are opened. Validation may select a specification and conformal calibration may set an error radius, but neither may use final-test outcomes. The professional-text archive uses locally cached Federal Reserve Economic Data rather than point-in-time historical vintages; revisions to an underlying macroeconomic series are therefore not reconstructed. Accordingly, this experiment supports a semantic-measurement claim, not a claim about real-time trading performance or revision-free historical forecasting. The methodology also distinguishes discovery from confirmation . Candidate response phrases, semantic equivalence classes, and presentation forms may be explored during discovery because language permits many plausible representations. Once chosen, however, these choices become part of the measurement procedure. 18 Altering them after seeing final-test errors would be analogous to changing a laboratory scale after observing the desired result. Prespecified partitions preserve the interpretation of held-out evidence. The empirical protocol follows the mathematical dependencies rather than presenting an undifferenti-ated collection of summaries. The semantic partition and presentation rule are selected before confirmatory measurement. Candidate phrases are checked for shared beginnings, the need to evaluate complete phrases, missing mass, and semantic ambiguity. Entire scenarios are assigned to discovery, semantic validation, cali-bration, model selection, conformal calibration, or final testing. The final-test partition remains inaccessible until the calibration rule, reference state, candidate set, and analysis are fixed. The static experiment targets posterior-space coverage, including interior compositions, near-boundary compositions, matched-entropy cases with different directions, and equal-modal-state cases with different residual mass. Affine calibration is the primary model because its smallest singular value has a transparent observability interpretation. More flexible function classes may be selected only on validation data. The reported diagnostics are expressed semantic mass, candidate-mass coverage, agreement between independent probability extractions, held-out additive log-ratio error, Jensen–Shannon divergence, proper scores, scenario-clustered confidence intervals, and split-conformal individual-scenario sets. The semantic-orientation experiment addresses a related but different question: whether language-derived probabilities agree with a fixed semantic reference protocol more reliably than probabilities explicitly printed by the same model. This is a comparison of two observable measurement channels under identical records, responses and partitions. The controlled posterior experiment supplies a complementary mathematical target because its reference posterior follows exactly from the declared generator.

The design supports three levels of conclusion. The paired semantic study asks whether two observable measurement channels differ under a common reference. The controlled study asks whether calibrated lexical coordinates recover an exact posterior out of sample and with advertised finite-sample coverage. The lexical-presentation and extraction studies are diagnostics: they locate sensitivity to wording, missing evidence and probability calculation. The archive records model versions, observation times, sampling settings, observed and normalised probability mass, presentation rules, scenario partitions and ran-dom seeds. The submission materials will include the fixed presentations, semantic definitions, scenario lists, probability tables, analysis programs and figure data. Since a fitted language distribution may change after publication, exact reproduction applies to the archived observations; new observations constitute statistical replications. The natural-language corpus consists of deterministic short-form market commentary compiled from Federal Reserve Economic Data series covering the S&P 500, CBOE VIX, two- and ten-year Treasury yields, and investment-grade and high-yield corporate credit spreads. It fixes US broad-equity risk orientation over the next five trading days as its case-study target and uses Risk-on, Mixed, Risk-off, and Unsure as the possible responses. The reference protocol and evaluation partition were frozen before confirmatory analysis. The paired comparison therefore estimates relative performance against the same prespecified semantic target 20 rather than allowing either measurement channel to define its own success criterion. The reference generator is deliberately domain-general. A finite Bayesian network is useful when evidence components have a known conditional structure, but it is neither mandatory nor automatically appropriate. A medical study might use an expert-reviewed diagnostic model; a reliability study might use a hidden Markov degradation model; a scientific-hypothesis study might use a finite likelihood family. The essential methodological requirement is that the reference posterior be reproducible and independent of the LLM measurement being evaluated. Sequential assessment requires a separate trajectory design. It must record both total lexical movement and movement relative to the declared transition, estimate the contraction coefficient, and test empirical coverage of Theorem 4. Because the current results establish posterior recovery from a fixed information set, the requirements in Section 2.2.4 remain outside the present empirical claim rather than being inferred from fixed-information accuracy. Together, these design choices make the empirical claims narrower but more reusable. The semantic-orientation study asks whether continuation-derived and numerically elicited probabilities agree with a se-mantic reference; the controlled-posterior study asks whether the former preserve known reference posterior distributions in log-ratio coordinates. Neither study alone establishes sequential validity. Their role is to establish a statistical measurement on which a dynamic experiment can subsequently be built.

Taken together, the procedures answer complementary questions. Paired tests compare measurement channels; bootstrap intervals quantify population-average uncertainty under scenario resampling; conformal calibration addresses uncertainty for a new scenario; singular values probe fitted inverse conditioning; and ablations test whether the channel responds to the information it is supposed to use. Agreement across these checks is more persuasive than any one statistic. Disagreement is also diagnostic: it indicates whether a failure arises from numerical elicitation, repeat variability, inverse conditioning, presentation sensitivity or a mismatch between word-level and state-level uncertainty.

3.2.1 Presentation perturbations and evidence sensitivity 

The perturbation theory separates two properties that an informative measurement must possess. Equivalent presentations should not create large changes, whereas removal of relevant evidence should matter. The first is an upper-stability question; the second protects against the degenerate solution in which the output barely changes because it does not use the evidence. The validation study was designed to examine both properties without selecting the final calibration on test outcomes. 24 This experiment operationalizes the paper’s central asymmetry. Phrase probabilities vary with both evidence curation and prompt presentation. Estimates obtained from information-preserving prompts are nevertheless judged against the same reference posterior because their evidence and reference model are unchanged. The method does not assume exact prompt invariance; it estimates the remaining presentation error and accepts only the prompt family for which that error is sufficiently controlled. Evidence omis-sions and contradictions are analyzed separately as curation or information-set changes rather than wording changes. The validation-only presentation experiment separates harmless variation in wording from consequential changes in the measurement. Twenty-four scenarios, balanced across three entropy strata, were evaluated twice under two information-preserving rewrites and three omissions. Reworded prose remained within the prespecified Jensen–Shannon margin 93.8% of the time for GPT-4.1-mini and 100% for GPT-4o-mini. Placing the evidence before the state definitions reduced these rates to 70.8% and 81.3%, showing that information equivalence does not imply measurement equivalence. Removing evidence increased reference Jensen–Shannon error by 0.173 [0 .105 , 0.244] and 0.200 [0 .133 , 0.268] , respectively. Removing the prior or detailed definitions did not yield a statistically clear mean degradation in this small study. Evidence is indispensable, while order is a nuisance factor that must remain fixed within the declared family of presentations. These findings are finite-design estimates of the two moduli introduced above rather than universal robustness constants. The high rewrite-equivalence rates suggest a small nuisance effect for the tested paraphrases, but the lower rates after reordering show that the admissible equivalence class cannot simply be defined as “prompts containing the same facts.” The evidence-omission intervals exclude zero and therefore supply the complementary sensitivity result: the calibrated output is not merely invariant; it responds when relevant information is removed. The study does not observe a hidden contextual representation and cannot say how far such a representation moved. It establishes the statistically relevant consequence—how much the observable probability composition and its posterior error changed. A prespecified extension examined two additional interventions on 12 validation scenarios per fitted model, balanced with four scenarios from each posterior-entropy stratum and repeated twice. The irrel-evant condition appended an administrative sentence with no bearing on the reference experiment. The contradiction condition replaced one observed variable by the alternative outcome that maximized the exact reference-posterior Jensen–Shannon change. This rule was deterministic and used the reference generator rather than the measured lexical probabilities. For GPT-4.1-mini, the irrelevant distractor produced mean total variation 0.047 with 95% scenario-bootstrap interval [0 .001 , 0.127] in the constrained response law. and 0.0084 , with intervals [0 .0002 , 0.212] and [0 .0027 , 0.0176] . These changes are small on average but not zero, and the total-variation intervals are wide because the experiment contains only 12 scenarios. # 4 Conclusion 

This paper asked whether probabilities over language, shaped by both the evidence supplied and its prompt, can support posterior inference about a finite-valued latent state. The answer is conditional but substantive. The target posterior depends on the specified evidence and reference model, but not merely on how fixed evidence is reworded; language probabilities often depend on both. Once meaningful states and their verbal realizations are declared, the response law induces a probability vector, and held-out calibration can test whether this measurement recovers the target. The resulting semiparametric construction requires no claim about inaccessible internal reasoning. It treats language as a stochastic measurement and asks the same questions that would be asked of any measurement procedure: what is the estimand, does the measurement exist, what error enters it, is the inverse identifiable, and does uncertainty assessment remain valid on new observations? The theory supplies corresponding answers. Semantic coarsening gives existence and conditional unique-ness. The approximation theorem bounds the effect of semantic mismatch, probability error and uncovered 26 mass. The lexical-presentation theorem shows when changes in wording, order or numerical format remain controlled. The inverse theorem identifies the condition needed for stable posterior recovery from a fixed information set, while the filtering theorem shows how error propagates as new evidence arrives through suc-cessive prompts. Together, these results explain both success and failure. A concentrated language-derived vector is not self-validating: it should be rejected when the verbal states cover too little probability, when the inverse is poorly conditioned, or when predictive coverage fails. The empirical results show that these conditions are attainable rather than merely formal. On professional text, language-derived probabilities outperform probabilities printed directly by the same fitted models. In the controlled experiment, two fitted language models recover exact posteriors on untouched scenarios and attain the stated conformal coverage. Ordinary paraphrases produce largely stable estimates of the common target, whereas reordering remains consequential and therefore cannot be treated as harmless by assumption. Removal or contradiction of relevant evidence changes the estimate in the scientifically expected direction. The structured response law also passes the prespecified probability-mass audit. These findings support the paper’s central claim on the sampled domains: useful posterior information is present in the native distribution over verbal responses and can be recovered by semantic grouping and calibration, although presentation error remains part of the uncertainty budget. The qualifications are equally informative. Presentation independence belongs to the reference target, not automatically to its language-derived estimate. Raw entropy over candidate words does not measure posterior entropy, lexical order can matter even when the stated facts do not change, and top-k probabilities do not identify the unrestricted distribution over all possible prose. Nor is the target independent of the evidence-generating assumptions: changing the evidence, state definition or reference model may legitimately change P (S | E). The method therefore does not turn every language output into a posterior and does not establish that a fitted model “knows” the truth. It supplies an auditable acceptance test for a declared state space, prompt family, fitted model and target population. When that test succeeds, the estimate can support abstention, requests for additional evidence, human review and other uncertainty-aware procedures; when it fails, the correct response is repair or rejection rather than false precision. For statistics, the broader implication extends beyond uncertainty quantification. A prompt-engineered response is still an observation from a wording-dependent language distribution; optimizing its surface form does not establish that its scientific meaning, recommendation or decision-relevant content is stable. The probability setting studied here makes that distinction explicit and testable, but the semantic-map principle is more general: declare the application-relevant meanings, group equivalent verbal realizations, quantify what is left unmatched, and validate the resulting measurement on new observations. Language-model uncertainty can then be formulated as a semiparametric latent-variable measurement problem and studied using calibration, inverse-problem stability, proper scoring, predictive coverage and filtering. Further work should establish finite-sample observability bounds for richer calibration classes, replicate the analysis across state definitions, populations and fitted models, and test semantic maps for classifications, recommendations and other consequential response types. Those extensions should preserve the order established here: first validate meaning-level recovery from a fixed information set, then study its dynamics, and only then allow it to inform consequential sequential decisions. 

## Limitations and responsible interpretation 

Lexical measurement can reveal uncertainty that a fluent answer or rounded numerical report conceals. Appropriate uses include abstention, requests for additional evidence, and prioritisation of human review. The method can mislead if conditional calibration is advertised as proof that a model “knows” the truth, if the semantic partition excludes clinically or socially meaningful alternatives, or if results are transferred across presentations, populations or fitted models. High-consequence users should retain domain review, monitor coverage over time, document the decision costs attached to every state, and provide a safe alternative outside the validated domain. The experiments use public or generated market commentary rather than private personal records, and the paper makes no claim of suitability for direct medical, legal, employment, or credit decisions. Future studies involving personal or sensitive data require appropriate privacy review, data governance, and, where applicable, institutional ethics approval. The method evaluates a probabilistic measurement; it does not authorise delegation of the underlying decision. 27 Data availability 

The prespecified presentations, scenario lists, observed probability tables, analysis programs, and figure data will be deposited with the accepted article or in a persistent public repository.
